Time series & forecasting

Businesses run on forward projections: inventory planning, staffing schedules, infrastructure capacity, and revenue targets. Time series forecasting breaks temporal data down into trend, seasonality, and residuals to make honest predictions with realistic confidence intervals while catching operational anomalies before they escalate.

In this area

    Forecasting requires treating time as an ordered, non-random axis. The diagram below illustrates how raw time series are decomposed, benchmarked against naive baselines, modeled with statistical algorithms (ARIMA/ETS), and validated through chronological backtesting.

    11.1 · Deconstructing time

    Trend, seasonality & noise

    A raw time series graph is a composite of multiple distinct behavioral forces. Decomposing the series isolates underlying momentum from calendar cycles and random noise.

    The Classical Decomposition Framework

    A time series \(Y_t\) can be decomposed into:

    • Trend-Cycle (\(T_t\)): The long-term progression or direction of the series over multiple years, reflecting organic growth, adoption, or decline.
    • Seasonality (\(S_t\)): Repeating fluctuations influenced by calendar factors with a known, fixed period (e.g. daily day-of-week dips on weekends, annual holiday retail spikes in Q4).
    • Residual / Noise (\(R_t\)): Irregular, unpredictable random variations remaining after removing trend and seasonality.

    Additive vs Multiplicative Models

    • Additive: \(Y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t\). Used when seasonal swings remain roughly constant in magnitude regardless of whether the trend is high or low.
    • Multiplicative: \(Y_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t\). Used when the amplitude of seasonal fluctuations grows proportionally as the overall level of the series rises.

    11.2 · Filtering noise

    Moving averages & smoothing

    Smoothing techniques average out short-term volatility to reveal underlying momentum and generate straightforward, responsive baseline forecasts.

    Moving Averages

    A simple moving average calculates the arithmetic mean of the last \(k\) periods. While effective for filtering noise, simple moving averages give equal weight to events yesterday and events 30 days ago, lagging behind rapid trend shifts.

    Exponential Smoothing Family

    • Simple Exponential Smoothing (SES): Assigns exponentially decreasing weights to past observations via smoothing parameter \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\): $$\hat{y}_{t+1} = \alpha y_t + (1 - \alpha)\hat{y}_t$$
    • Holt's Linear Trend: Adds a slope equation with parameter \(\beta\) to model ongoing linear growth or decline.
    • Holt-Winters Triple Smoothing: Extends Holt's model with a seasonal equation parameter \(\gamma\), updating level, trend, and repeating seasonal patterns simultaneously.

    11.3 · Honest benchmarks

    Baseline forecasts

    Before training machine learning algorithms or tuning ARIMA hyperparameters, you must evaluate simple heuristic baselines. If a complex model cannot comfortably beat a seasonal naïve baseline, deploying it is an expensive operational liability.

    Essential Baseline Methods

    • Naïve Forecast (Persistence): Assumes the next period will equal the most recent period: \(\hat{y}_{t+h} = y_t\). Extremely effective for financial random walks.
    • Seasonal Naïve: Sets the forecast equal to the value from the identical season of the previous cycle: \(\hat{y}_{t+h} = y_{t+h-m}\) (e.g. this Tuesday will equal last Tuesday).
    • Drift Method: Extrapolates the average historical change over time from the beginning to the end of the observed history.

    The Benchmark Discipline

    Always compute the Mean Absolute Scaled Error (MASE). MASE divides your model's MAE by the MAE of a simple seasonal naïve baseline. If \(\text{MASE} \ge 1.0\), your model performs worse than simply repeating last year's numbers!

    11.4 · Statistical time series modeling

    ETS & ARIMA

    ARIMA and ETS are the twin pillars of classical statistical forecasting. They describe autocorrelation structures in stationary series to produce mathematically rigorous forecasts with prediction intervals.

    Stationarity and Differencing

    ARIMA models require the series to be stationary: its mean, variance, and autocovariance must remain constant over time. Non-stationary trends are eliminated through differencing (\(y'_t = y_t - y_{t-1}\)).

    The Anatomy of ARIMA(p, d, q)

    • \(p\) (Autoregressive - AR): Regresses the variable against its own past lag values: \(\phi_1 y_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_p y_{t-p}\).
    • \(d\) (Integrated - I): The order of differencing applied to make the series stationary.
    • \(q\) (Moving Average - MA): Regresses against past forecast residual errors: \(\theta_1 \epsilon_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q \epsilon_{t-q}\).

    For seasonal data, SARIMA\((p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_m\) incorporates seasonal lag terms matching the cycle length \(m\).

    11.5 · Validating predictions

    Backtesting & forecast error

    Evaluating a time series model requires forward rolling cross-validation (backtesting). Measuring forecast accuracy across multiple test horizons ensures your models remain dependable when deployed.

    Core Error Metrics

    • MAE (Mean Absolute Error): \(\frac{1}{n}\sum |y_t - \hat{y}_t|\). Intuitive and directly expressed in business units (e.g. ±$150).
    • RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error): \(\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum (y_t - \hat{y}_t)^2}\). Squares errors before averaging, penalizing catastrophic forecast misses heavily.
    • MAPE (Mean Absolute Percentage Error): \(\frac{1}{n}\sum \left|\frac{y_t - \hat{y}_t}{y_t}\right|\). Expressed as a percentage, but explodes when actuals are near zero.
    • WAPE (Weighted Absolute Percentage Error): \(\frac{\sum |y_t - \hat{y}_t|}{\sum y_t}\). Stable across intermittent demand and zero values.

    Data Leakage via Shuffling

    Never apply standard scikit-learn train_test_split(shuffle=True) to time series. Shuffling allows models to peek at tomorrow's data to predict yesterday, yielding unrealistically perfect accuracy that collapses in production.

    11.6 · Operational alerting

    Anomaly detection

    Anomalies are unexpected events that deviate significantly from expected temporal behavior. Effective monitoring separates genuine system outages from normal seasonal variations.

    Dynamic Bounds vs Static Thresholds

    Static thresholds (e.g. "alert if traffic drops below 5,000 requests/sec") fail because normal traffic is lower at 3:00 AM than at 2:00 PM. Dynamic anomaly detection models expected value \(\hat{y}_t\) and constructs confidence bands:

    $$\text{Alert if } y_t \notin \left[\hat{y}_t - 3\sigma_t, \; \hat{y}_t + 3\sigma_t\right]$$

    Decomposition-Based Anomaly Detection

    By fitting an STL decomposition, you remove predictable weekly seasonality and long-term trend. Outlier detection is then performed on the isolated residuals \(R_t\), catching subtle percentage drops during peak hours that static alerts completely miss.
