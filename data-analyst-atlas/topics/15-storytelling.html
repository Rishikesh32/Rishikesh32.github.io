Communication & storytelling

An analysis only counts when someone decides something differently because of it. This area covers the half of the job that happens after the query runs: framing the question, writing the summary, presenting the chart, bringing people along and making a recommendation you can defend.

In this area

    Communication is not a coat of paint you add at the end. It starts before the first query, when you turn a vague request into a decision with a metric and a deadline, and it ends when the decision is made. In between you write a summary that leads with the answer, present a few slides whose titles carry the message, keep a written doc others can check, work with the people who decide, and package the result as a recommendation with confidence and next steps. One running example ties the lessons together: a meal-kit subscription app whose monthly churn rose from 6.1% to 7.4% (illustrative numbers).

    15.1 · Before the first query

    Framing the question

    Most requests arrive as a topic: "can you look into churn?" A topic has no finish line, so the analysis grows until someone loses interest. Framing turns the topic into a question with a finish line: the decision it informs, who makes it, the metric that settles it, the result that would change the choice, and the date the answer is needed by.

    Why it matters for analysts

    Unframed work is the main reason good analysis goes unused. Ten minutes of framing at intake tells you which tables matter, how precise the answer must be, and when "good enough" arrives. It also gives you a polite way to push back on requests that no decision depends on.

    From topic to decision

    Ask the requester one question first: what will you do differently depending on the answer? If nothing would change, the request is curiosity, which is fine, but it gets a smaller budget. If something would change, you now know the decision, and the rest follows from it.

    The metric must be one that can move the decision. Total monthly churn cannot tell you whether to change the promo; churn of promo signups compared with full-price signups can. The threshold says in advance which result means "act", so nobody reinterprets the finding afterwards to fit what they already believed.

    Question framer Try it

    A decision such as "understand churn better" scores nothing: it has no finish line. Notice that the metric and threshold only count when they match the decision you picked. A churn-risk score is a fine metric for deciding who gets a retention call and a useless one for deciding about the promo.

    Break the question into a hypothesis tree

    Once the question is framed, split "why did churn rise?" into branches that do not overlap and together cover every explanation. Consultants call this MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive). Each leaf is a hypothesis you can test with one query, so the tree doubles as your work plan.

    Always include a "measurement changed" branch. A new tracking event, a redefined "active customer" or a pipeline bug can move a metric as easily as customers can.

    Intake questions to ask every time

    AskWhyExample answer
    What will you do differently based on the answer?Finds the decisionChange the promo, or not
    Who makes that call, and who else must agree?Finds the owner and the stakeholdersVP of Growth; Finance signs off on discounts
    When is the decision made?Sets the deadline and the precision you can affordPlanning meeting on 20 Oct

    Key ideas
    • A framed question names a decision, an owner, a metric, a threshold and a deadline.
    • Ask "what will you do differently?" first; every other choice follows from the decision.
    • The metric and threshold must fit the decision, and the threshold is set before you look.
    • A MECE hypothesis tree turns the question into a list of testable queries.
    • Always keep a "measurement changed" branch in the tree.
    Common mistakes
    • Starting the query before anyone has named the decision, then answering the wrong question very precisely.
    • Accepting "understand X better" as a goal. It has no finish line.
    • Choosing the threshold after seeing the results, which lets everyone read the finding their own way.
    • Building a hypothesis tree with overlapping branches, so the same query "confirms" two causes.

    15.2 · Answer first

    Executive summaries

    You did the work in order: background, data, method, findings, answer. Your reader wants it in the opposite order. An executive summary leads with the answer, follows with the two or three facts that justify it, and ends with what you need from the reader. This is called BLUF, bottom line up front.

    Why it matters for analysts

    Senior readers skim. Many will read your first two sentences and stop, often on a phone between meetings. If the answer is in sentence six, most of them never see it, and the decision gets made without your analysis.

    Flip the order: the pyramid

    Barbara Minto's pyramid principle puts the single main point at the top. Under it sit a few supporting arguments, and under each argument sits its evidence. The reader can stop at any level and still leave with the right conclusion.

    The chronological version feels natural to write because it mirrors your memory of the project. It makes the reader relive your three weeks before learning what you found.

    Five sentences that carry the decision

    A reliable shape for a summary is five sentences: the recommendation, the evidence behind it, the expected impact, the main risk, and the ask (what you need, from whom, by when). Method details go last or into the appendix. A reader who wants to check your work will keep reading; a reader who only needs the answer can stop after one line.

    BLUF ordering game Try it
    Use ↑ and ↓ to reorder the summary.

      The bars show a typical skim pattern: each extra sentence loses some readers (an illustrative curve, not a measured one). Wherever the recommendation sits, only the readers who get that far see it. Move it to the top and every reader leaves with the answer.

      Count the cost of burying the lead

      Even with made-up numbers, the shape is the point. Attention drops fast after the first lines, so the expected number of readers who learn your conclusion depends mostly on where you put it.

      <!-- Five-sentence executive summary template -->
**Recommendation:** Cap the first-box promo at 30% off and require a second box, starting with the 1 Nov campaign.
**Why:** Promo signups churn 14% in month 2 vs 6% for full price, and promo share of signups rose from 20% to 45%.
**Impact:** Monthly churn should fall from 7.4% to about 6.6% (range 6.4% to 6.9%).
**Risk:** Signups may drop 10 to 15% with the smaller discount, so we propose a 4-week test first.
**Ask:** Approve the test by 20 Oct so results arrive before the campaign.

<!-- Method, data sources and caveats go below, or in the appendix -->
      Key ideas
      • BLUF: put the recommendation in the first sentence.
      • The pyramid: one main point, a few supporting arguments, evidence underneath.
      • Recommendation, evidence, impact, risk, ask: five sentences cover most decisions.
      • Method goes last or in an appendix; it serves the checkers, not the deciders.
      • Specific numbers beat adjectives: "14% vs 6%" instead of "much higher".
      Common mistakes
      • Opening with "We analysed 18 months of data using…" instead of the answer.
      • Listing findings without saying what to do about them.
      • Hedging every sentence until the reader cannot tell what you recommend.
      • Forgetting the ask, so the reader agrees and nothing happens.
      In practice

      Write the summary first, as a hypothesis, on day one. Update it as the analysis proceeds. If you cannot write the first sentence by the end, you probably do not have a finding yet.

      15.3 · Slides that say something

      Presenting insights

      A slide titled "Churn by acquisition offer" tells the audience what the chart is about and leaves them to work out what it means. A slide titled "Promo signups churn 2.3× faster than full-price signups" tells them what to see. The chart then becomes evidence for a sentence, and everyone in the room reads it the same way.

      Why it matters for analysts

      In a meeting you get a few minutes and a lot of competing opinions. If each slide makes one claim in its title, the discussion stays on your conclusion instead of on what the axes mean, and people who only flip through the deck later still get the message.

      Anatomy of a slide that lands

      Each slide carries one message. The action title (also called a takeaway title or headline) states it as a full sentence with a number. The chart shows the evidence, with the series that matters in colour and everything else in grey. An annotation puts the key number where the eye lands, and a footnote gives the source and definitions so nobody has to ask.

      Slide headline rewriter Try it

      Type a title and watch the checks: a verb, a number, the segment the chart is about, a comparison, and a length that fits on one or two lines. When your title names the promo segment, the chart greys out the other bars, because the title has told the audience where to look.

      Three slides in three minutes

      For a decision meeting, plan the talk backwards from the ask. Twenty seconds of context, a minute on the one insight, a minute on the recommendation and its impact, and the last forty seconds on what you need and by when. Everything else goes into an appendix you open only when someone asks.

      Key ideas
      • One slide, one message, stated in an action title with a number.
      • Colour the evidence and grey out the context.
      • Annotate the key number on the chart, using the title's words.
      • Plan the talk backwards from the ask; keep the main deck to a few slides.
      • An appendix lets you answer detailed questions without making everyone sit through them.
      Common mistakes
      • Topic titles that leave each person in the room to draw a different conclusion.
      • Showing every chart you made because each one cost you effort.
      • Reading the slide aloud instead of saying what it means.
      • Running out of time before the ask, so the meeting ends without a decision.
      In practice

      Read only your slide titles in order, out loud. If they tell the whole story on their own, the deck works. If they don't, fix the titles before you touch the charts.

      15.4 · The written record

      Writing analysis docs

      Slides are for the meeting; the analysis doc is for everything after it. A good doc lets three different readers get what they need: the executive reads the first paragraph, the manager reads the findings, and a peer analyst checks the method and reruns the queries. One document serves all three if you put things in the right order.

      Why it matters for analysts

      Six months from now someone will ask why the promo changed. The doc is the answer. It also protects you: when your numbers are questioned, a doc with definitions, sources and queries lets anyone check them in minutes instead of reopening the whole project.

      Layer the doc by reader

      Put the TL;DR (too long; didn't read: a three-to-five sentence summary) and the question at the top. Findings and the recommendation follow, each finding stated as a sentence with its chart. Limitations stay in the main body because they change how much weight the reader should give the result. Method details, full queries and the twenty exploratory charts you made along the way go to the appendix.

      Analysis doc outline checker Try it

      Each section has a place: Top, Body, Appendix or Omit. The default outline is the order the work happened in, with method first and the queries pasted into the body. Fix it so the reader reaches the answer in seconds and the main doc reads in under eight minutes, without dropping anything a reviewer needs.

      Make every number traceable

      Every number in the doc should lead back to a query or notebook a peer can rerun. Link each chart to the query that produced it, state the date the data was pulled, and write the metric definition next to the first use. "Churn" alone is ambiguous; "cancelled before the third box" is not.

      -- churn_by_offer.sql  (appendix A, figure 2)
-- Question: month-2 churn by acquisition offer, signups Jan 2025 to Jun 2026
-- Definition: churned = cancelled before the third box was shipped
-- Data pulled: 2026-10-06 from analytics.subscriptions
SELECT
    acquisition_offer,
    COUNT(*)                                             AS signups,
    AVG(CASE WHEN boxes_shipped < 3 AND cancelled_at IS NOT NULL
             THEN 1.0 ELSE 0.0 END)                       AS month2_churn
FROM analytics.subscriptions
WHERE signup_date >= DATE '2025-01-01'
  AND signup_date <  DATE '2026-07-01'
GROUP BY acquisition_offer
ORDER BY month2_churn DESC;
      Key ideas
      • Order the doc by reader: TL;DR and question first, findings and recommendation next, method and queries last.
      • Limitations belong in the main body because they change how much to trust the result.
      • Every number links to a query, a data date and a written definition.
      • Exploratory charts are part of the work, not the doc; keep them in the appendix or drop them.
      Common mistakes
      • A doc that reads like a lab notebook: everything you tried, in the order you tried it.
      • Undefined metrics, so two readers compute "churn" two ways and disagree.
      • Screenshots of numbers with no query behind them.
      • Hiding the limitations in an appendix nobody opens.

      15.5 · People, not just data

      Working with stakeholders

      A stakeholder is anyone who can affect your analysis or is affected by its result. The VP who asked, the CFO who signs off on discounts, and the campaign manager whose promo is about to be cut all react differently to the same finding. Knowing who they are, what they care about and when to talk to them decides whether your recommendation survives the meeting.

      Why it matters for analysts

      Analyses rarely fail in the query. They fail when a senior person sees a surprising number for the first time in a group meeting and spends the hour questioning it, or when the person whose work looks bad hears it in public. Both are avoidable with a little planning.

      Map influence against interest

      Place each stakeholder on two axes: influence (how much they can change the decision) and interest (how much they care about the outcome). The quadrant tells you how to engage. High influence and high interest: manage closely, involve them from framing onwards. High influence, low interest: keep satisfied with short, conclusion-first updates. Low influence, high interest: keep informed. Low on both: monitor and send the final doc.

      Stakeholder map Try it

      All five stakeholders start on the centre lines, which means "not yet thought about". Read each profile, then move the person into the quadrant that matches it. A point sitting exactly on a line counts as undecided.

      Pre-wire: no surprises in the meeting

      Before the decision meeting, walk the key people through the finding one at a time. The CFO sees the impact estimate and raises her question about the revenue risk in private, so you can add the answer to the deck. The campaign manager hears that her promo is the cause from you, early, and can help design the fix. By the time the group meets, nobody hears the main number for the first time.

      Who does what: a RACI for the decision

      A RACI chart lists who is Responsible for the work, who is Accountable for the decision (exactly one person), who is Consulted before it and who is Informed after it. Agreeing it at kickoff prevents the late "why wasn't I asked?" that reopens a decision.

      TaskAnalystVP of GrowthCFOCampaign manager
      Frame the questionRAIC
      Run the analysisR, AIIC
      Decide on the promoCACC
      Run the 4-week testR (measure)AIR (launch)
      Key ideas
      • Map stakeholders by influence and interest; the quadrant sets how you engage.
      • Involve the decision owner from framing onwards, not just at the readout.
      • Pre-wire: show key people the finding one-to-one before the group meeting.
      • Tell people whose work looks bad early and privately, and invite them into the fix.
      • Agree who is accountable for the decision at kickoff.
      Common mistakes
      • Disappearing for three weeks and returning with a finished deck nobody asked to see early.
      • Sending a high-influence, low-interest executive a 20-page doc instead of three sentences.
      • Letting the loudest person in the meeting redefine the question halfway through.
      • Treating disagreement as an attack. It usually points to a definition or an assumption you can check together.
      In practice

      When a stakeholder pushes back on a number, ask "what would you expect it to be, and why?" Their answer often reveals a different definition or a known data issue, and you can resolve it with a query instead of an argument.

      15.6 · Ask for the decision

      Making recommendations

      A finding says what is true. A recommendation says what to do about it, how much it should help, how sure you are, and what happens next. Analysts who stop at findings leave the hardest step to someone with less context. Analysts who recommend, with honest uncertainty, become the people decision makers call first.

      Why it matters for analysts

      Decision makers rarely lack data; they lack a clear option with its trade-offs spelled out. A recommendation with an impact range, a confidence level and a dated next step can be approved in one meeting. A list of findings usually produces a request for more analysis.

      The parts of a recommendation

      State the action first. Give the expected impact as a range, not a single number, because a point estimate hides how uncertain you are. Say your confidence in plain words and what it rests on. Name the main risk and the alternatives you rejected. End with the next step, an owner and a date, plus the result that would make you change your mind.

      Compare options by range, not by point

      Put every serious option on the same scale with its plausible range. An option with a higher best guess can still be the riskier choice if its range crosses zero. Removing the promo entirely might add more revenue, or it might lose some; capping it is smaller but positive across the whole range.

      Match the action to confidence and reversibility

      How sure you need to be depends on how easy the decision is to undo. A reversible change with a positive expected value can roll out with a holdout group and a review date. A change that is hard to reverse, such as a price announced publicly, deserves a test first when confidence is only medium. Low confidence means gather more evidence before recommending anything.

      Recommendation builder Try it

      The expected annual value is confidence × midpoint impact × 12 months, minus the one-off cost (example figures). Watch how the verdict changes when you untick "easy to reverse" at the same confidence, and how a cheap option can still be a bad recommendation when its expected value is below its cost.

      HighRandomized test, or a large effect replicated across cohorts"We expect…", "The test showed…"

      Key ideas
      • A recommendation states the action, impact range, confidence, risk, alternatives and next step.
      • Compare options by their ranges; a range that crosses zero is a gamble.
      • Reversible decisions can move faster; hard-to-reverse ones need more confidence or a test.
      • Say in advance what result would change your mind.
      • Every next step needs an owner and a date.
      Common mistakes
      • Presenting findings and waiting for someone else to say what they mean.
      • A single impact number with false precision, such as "+$38,412 a month".
      • Overclaiming causality from an observational pattern.
      • Ending with "let me know your thoughts" instead of a specific ask.

      Put it together

      Projects for this area

      Starter

      Rewrite a past analysis

      Take an analysis you already did (a class project or a Kaggle notebook). Write a five-sentence executive summary that leads with the answer, and rewrite every chart title as a takeaway. Compare reading time before and after.

      Intermediate

      Framed question to 3-slide readout

      Pick a public dataset (for example a bike-share or e-commerce dataset). Write a one-page brief with the decision, metric, deadline and threshold, run the analysis, then deliver a 3-slide, 3-minute readout with an appendix. Record yourself and time it.

      Stretch

      Full analysis doc with recommendation

      Write a complete analysis doc: TL;DR, question, findings, a recommendation with an impact range and confidence level, limitations and next steps, plus reproducible queries in the appendix. Ask a friend to play the skeptical stakeholder and log every question they ask.
