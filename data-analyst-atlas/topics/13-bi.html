BI & dashboards

A dashboard is a small product: it answers a few recurring questions for a specific audience, on top of a data model, a handful of calculations and a refresh schedule. This area covers how to design, model, calculate, speed up and launch dashboards in Power BI, Tableau or Looker so that people trust the numbers and come back every week.

In this area

    Every dashboard in this area is the same weekly sales dashboard for an online store, built from an orders table. The business questions decide the grain of the data model and the layout of the page. The model holds facts and dimensions, measures turn them into numbers, and filters let people slice those numbers. Refresh settings decide how fresh and how fast it all is, and usage tells you whether anyone cares. The six lessons follow that path, and the loop at the top is why a dashboard is never finished on launch day.

    13.1 · Questions first, then pixels

    Dashboard design

    A good dashboard answers three to five recurring questions for one audience, in the order they ask them. People read a screen from the top left, so the most important number goes there, the trend that explains it comes next, and the detail table goes last. Design is mostly deciding what to leave out.

    Why it matters for data analysts

    Dashboards are often the most visible thing an analyst builds. A cluttered page with 20 charts gets opened once and then ignored, and the questions come back to your inbox as ad hoc requests. A focused page answers them before anyone asks.

    Start from the decision

    Before you open the BI tool, write down who will use the page, when, and what they will decide. "Sales leads, Monday morning, decide where to push this week" leads to three questions, and each question picks its own visual.

    Reading order and hierarchy

    Eye-tracking studies of screens show a "Z" or "F" scan: top left first, then across, then down. Put headline KPIs (key performance indicators) in a top row with their comparison (vs target, vs last year), then the trend, then breakdowns, and keep detail tables at the bottom or behind a drill-through.

    Dashboard layout critic Try it

    You can also click one tile and then another to swap them. The score counts how many pairs of tiles appear in the right order: KPIs before the trend, the trend before the breakdown, and the breakdown before the detail table. Slots read left to right, top row first.

    Pick the visual for the question

    QuestionGood visualAvoid
    Are we on target?KPI tile with target and delta, or a bullet chartGauges and donuts that use a lot of space for one number
    Which way is it going?Line chart at the grain people manage (week, month)Daily noise when decisions are weekly

    Key ideas
    • Write the audience, the decision and three to five questions before you build anything.
    • One visual per question; delete charts that answer nothing on the list.
    • Top left holds the headline KPI with its comparison, then trend, then breakdown, then detail.
    • A number without context (target, last year, last week) can't tell anyone whether to act.
    • Use colour to flag the exception, not to decorate every bar.
    Common mistakes
    • Building one dashboard for every audience, so nobody finds their three numbers.
    • Putting a large detail table or a slicer wall in the most valuable top-left spot.
    • Showing KPIs without a comparison, or with a different date range from the charts below them.
    • Using a different colour for every category, so the one problem area doesn't stand out.

    13.2 · Star schemas

    Data models for BI

    BI tools work best on a star schema: one fact table of events (order lines) in the middle, and dimension tables (date, product, customer, store) around it that describe those events. Every relationship runs one-to-many from a dimension to the fact table, and filters flow the same way, from the dimension you slice by down to the facts you add up.

    Why it matters for data analysts

    Most "the dashboard is wrong" bugs are model bugs: a duplicated key, a missing relationship or two tables at different grains. Measures stay short and fast on a clean star, and become fragile workarounds on a flat export.

    Facts and dimensions

    A fact table holds one row per event at a stated grain ("one row per order line") with keys and numbers you add up. A dimension table holds one row per thing (product, customer, day) with the text attributes you group and filter by. The key in each dimension must be unique.

    Relationships and filter direction

    In Power BI you draw relationships in the model view; in Tableau you relate logical tables; in Looker you declare joins in an Explore. In all three, the "one" side must have unique keys. If a dimension key repeats, the tool either refuses the relationship, treats it as many-to-many, or (in a SQL join) silently multiplies fact rows.

    Star-schema builder Try it

    The fact table has 8 order lines worth 880 in total. dim_product was loaded twice for product P3, once with a stale category. Watch what an unrelated dimension does (in Power BI every row shows the grand total, because nothing filters the facts) and what a duplicated key does (rows add up to more than the total).

    Grain: one row per what?

    Trouble starts when you join two tables at different grains. Orders have one row per order; shipments have one row per parcel. Join them and an order sent in two parcels appears twice, so its revenue is counted twice. Keep each fact table at one grain and relate both to shared dimensions instead.

    -- 1. Check that a dimension key is unique before you relate it
SELECT product_key, COUNT(*) AS n
FROM dim_product
GROUP BY product_key
HAVING COUNT(*) > 1;          -- must return zero rows

-- 2. Check that the fact table really has the grain you claim
SELECT order_id, line_number, COUNT(*) AS n
FROM fct_sales
GROUP BY order_id, line_number
HAVING COUNT(*) > 1;

-- 3. A star query: slice facts by dimension attributes
SELECT p.category, s.region, SUM(f.net_amount) AS revenue
FROM fct_sales f
JOIN dim_product p ON p.product_key = f.product_key
JOIN dim_store   s ON s.store_key   = f.store_key
GROUP BY p.category, s.region;
    TableOne row perColumnsSize
    FactEvent at a fixed grain (order line, page view)Foreign keys, numeric measuresLong: millions of rows
    DimensionThing (product, customer, day)Unique key, descriptive textShort and wide
    Date dimensionCalendar dayWeek, month, fiscal period, holiday flagsAbout 365 rows per year
    Flat exportMixed: whatever the query producedEverythingAvoid as a BI model
    Key ideas
    • A star schema puts one fact table at a stated grain in the middle and dimensions around it.
    • Relationships go one-to-many from dimension to fact, and filters flow the same way.
    • Dimension keys must be unique; a duplicate turns the relationship many-to-many and inflates totals.
    • Never join two facts at different grains into one table; relate both to shared dimensions.
    • Use a proper date table so time intelligence (last year, year to date) works.
    Common mistakes
    • Importing one wide flat export and building every measure around its duplicates.
    • Leaving a dimension unrelated, so a slicer on it shows the same total on every row.
    • Setting relationships to filter in both directions "to make it work", which creates ambiguous paths and slow models.
    • Joining orders to shipments or returns before loading, which double counts revenue.

    13.3 · Filter context

    Measures & calculations

    A calculated column is computed once per row when the data refreshes and is stored like any other column. A measure is computed at query time, separately for every cell of every visual, using only the rows that cell's filters leave. Most BI logic belongs in measures, because the same formula then gives the right answer for a region, a week or the grand total.

    Why it matters for data analysts

    Ratios such as margin, conversion and average order value are where dashboards go quietly wrong. If you average a per-row ratio instead of dividing two sums, the total row is wrong and nobody notices until finance does.

    Filter context

    Every cell in a visual has a filter context: the combination of row and column headers, slicers and page filters that applies to it. The engine keeps the fact rows that match, then evaluates the measure on those rows. The total row has fewer filters, so it sees more rows.

    Ratio of sums, not average of ratios

    Margin for a group is total profit divided by total revenue. If you store a per-order margin column and average it, a $10 order counts as much as a $1,000 order. The two answers can differ a lot.

    Measure vs calculated column, evaluated Try it

    The left table is the model: 8 sales rows and a stored MarginPct column (profit / amount per row). The right table is the visual. Compare the total row of the two margin formulas, and work out why ALL(Region) behaves differently when the rows are channels.

    The same idea in three tools

    -- Power BI (DAX) measures
Revenue      = SUM ( Sales[NetAmount] )
Orders       = DISTINCTCOUNT ( Sales[OrderID] )
AOV          = DIVIDE ( [Revenue], [Orders] )        -- ratio of sums, safe divide
Margin %     = DIVIDE ( SUM ( Sales[Profit] ), [Revenue] )
Revenue LY   = CALCULATE ( [Revenue], SAMEPERIODLASTYEAR ( 'Date'[Date] ) )
YoY %        = DIVIDE ( [Revenue] - [Revenue LY], [Revenue LY] )
-- CALCULATE changes the filter context; ALL removes the Region filter
% of regions = DIVIDE ( [Revenue], CALCULATE ( [Revenue], ALL ( Sales[Region] ) ) )
    // Tableau calculated fields
// Margin % (aggregate calculation: evaluated per mark, like a measure)
SUM([Profit]) / SUM([Sales])

// First order date per customer, whatever is in the view (LOD expression)
{ FIXED [Customer ID] : MIN([Order Date]) }

// New vs returning revenue: compare each order to the customer's first order
IF [Order Date] = { FIXED [Customer ID] : MIN([Order Date]) }
THEN "New" ELSE "Returning" END

    • A measure is evaluated per cell in that cell's filter context; a calculated column is stored per row.
    • Build ratios as one sum divided by another so every level, including totals, is correct.
    • CALCULATE modifies filter context; ALL removes a filter for a denominator.
    • Tableau's FIXED LOD computes at a level you choose, independent of the view.
    • Define each business metric once, in the model, and reuse it everywhere.
    Common mistakes
    • Averaging a per-row ratio column instead of dividing two sums.
    • Using calculated columns for numbers that should be measures, which bloats the model and breaks totals.
    • Using / instead of DIVIDE and showing errors or infinity when the denominator is zero.
    • Copying the same "Revenue" logic into five reports with small differences in refund handling.

    13.4 · Slicing without breaking

    Filters & interactivity

    Interactivity lets one page serve many questions: pick a region, click a bar, drill from month to week. Every interaction is just a filter added to some visuals' filter context. Your job is to decide which visuals each filter should reach, and to make the current filters obvious on screen.

    Why it matters for data analysts

    A slicer that silently filters the company target tile, or a hidden page filter that excludes refunds, produces numbers that look right and aren't. Clear filter scopes are what make a self-service dashboard trustworthy.

    Where a filter lives

    Filters nest. A report filter applies to every page, a page filter to every visual on one page, and a visual filter to one chart. A slicer is a page filter that viewers control. Tableau has the same layers as data source, context and dimension filters; Looker uses dashboard filters wired to each tile.

    Cross-filter or cross-highlight

    Clicking a mark in one visual filters the others on the page. Power BI offers two styles per pair of visuals: filter (other charts redraw with only the selected data) and highlight (other charts keep their full bars and shade the selected part). You can also switch an interaction off for a visual that must ignore it.

    Filter and slicer interaction graph Try it

    By default every filter reaches every visual, which is also the default in most BI tools. You can click the product bars to cross-filter. The company target tile compares revenue with a company-wide target of $320k, so think about which filters should reach it.

    Drill-down, drill-through and clear state

    Drill-down moves along a hierarchy inside one visual (year → month → week). Drill-through jumps to a detail page carrying the clicked value as a filter, which is where the order table belongs. Whatever you add, show the active filters in a title, and give people a reset button.

    -- Dynamic title so viewers always see the active region
Page title =
"Weekly sales · " & SELECTEDVALUE ( Store[Region], "All regions" )

-- A tile that should ignore the region slicer: remove that filter inside the measure
Company revenue = CALCULATE ( [Revenue], ALL ( Store[Region] ) )
Company vs target = DIVIDE ( [Company revenue], [Company target] )

    Key ideas
    • Every interaction is a filter added to some visuals' filter context.
    • Filters nest: report, page, visual; slicers are page filters that viewers control.
    • Cross-filter redraws with only the selection; cross-highlight shades it inside the full total.
    • Turn interactions off, or remove filters in the measure, for tiles that must show company-wide numbers.
    • Always show the active filters, and keep detail tables on drill-through pages.
    Common mistakes
    • Leaving the default "everything filters everything", so a target or benchmark tile changes with the slicer.
    • Hiding important page filters that viewers can't see.
    • Adding ten slicers so the page becomes a query builder and nobody can reproduce a number.
    • Forgetting that a selection persists when the report is saved or shared.

    13.5 · Fast and fresh enough

    Performance & refresh

    Every BI tool makes the same trade. You can copy the data into the tool's own compressed store and refresh it on a schedule (fast pages, data as old as the last refresh), or you can send live SQL to the warehouse for every visual (fresh data, slower pages, more warehouse cost). Power BI calls these Import and DirectQuery; Tableau calls them extract and live.

    Why it matters for data analysts

    People abandon a page that takes ten seconds to load, and they stop trusting one that shows Tuesday's numbers on Thursday. Picking the storage mode, the refresh schedule and the number of visuals is part of the analyst's job, not an afterthought for IT.

    Import versus DirectQuery

    In Import mode the refresh runs a few big queries, compresses the result column by column and keeps it in memory, so each visual answers in milliseconds. In DirectQuery mode nothing is stored; each visual on each page view becomes a SQL query, so speed depends on the warehouse and the number of visuals.

    How old can the data get?

    With Import, data age climbs until the next refresh, then drops. The worst moment is just before a refresh, so the schedule matters more than the count: two refreshes at 06:00 and 14:00 still leave 16 hours between them overnight. Schedule refreshes right after the upstream pipeline finishes, not at a round hour.

    Import vs DirectQuery trade-off calculator Try it

    The cost model is illustrative but has the right shape. A full refresh re-reads every row (about 0.4 minutes per million here), while incremental refresh reloads only recent partitions. DirectQuery runs about four visual queries at a time, and each query slows down as the table grows.

    Make it faster

    Most slow dashboards have too many visuals or a model that is too big. Remove columns nobody uses, pre-aggregate in the warehouse, and keep high-cardinality columns such as order IDs out of the model unless a drill-through needs them.

    -- A daily aggregate for the dashboard: 200M order lines become ~70k rows
CREATE TABLE marts.agg_sales_daily AS
SELECT
    order_date,
    store_key,
    product_category,
    COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders,
    SUM(net_amount)          AS revenue,
    SUM(profit)              AS profit
FROM marts.fct_sales
GROUP BY order_date, store_key, product_category;
-- Point the KPI and trend visuals at this table; keep fct_sales for drill-through.
-- Store revenue and profit, not margin %, so margin can be rebuilt as a ratio of sums.

    Power BIImport (scheduled or incremental refresh)DirectQueryComposite models, aggregation tables
    TableauExtract (.hyper), scheduled refreshLive connectionIncremental extracts
    LookerNone: it always generates SQLEvery queryQuery caching, persistent derived tables, aggregate awareness
    Key ideas
    • Import is fast and stale between refreshes; DirectQuery is fresh, slower and costs warehouse compute on every view.
    • The worst data age is the longest gap between refreshes, plus refresh time.
    • Incremental refresh reloads only recent partitions, which makes frequent refreshes possible on big tables.
    • Fewer visuals and pre-aggregated tables speed up both modes.
    • Schedule the BI refresh after the upstream pipeline, not before it.
    Common mistakes
    • Choosing DirectQuery for "real-time" when the warehouse itself only loads once a night.
    • Refreshing at 05:00 when the pipeline finishes at 06:00, so the dashboard is a day behind all day.
    • Importing every column of a wide table, including IDs and free text nobody filters on.
    • Putting 25 visuals on one page and blaming the tool for the load time.

    13.6 · From launched to used

    Driving adoption

    A dashboard creates value only when someone uses it to decide something. Treat it like a product: know who it is for, launch it where those people already meet, measure who comes back, and fix or retire what nobody uses. Adoption is a funnel, and most people drop out after the first look.

    Why it matters for data analysts

    Usage data tells you whether your work changed anything. It also protects your time: a report with two viewers a month is a candidate to retire, and a page the sales team opens every Monday deserves your next improvement.

    The adoption funnel

    Count people at each stage: in the audience, opened it once, came back the next week, and use it weekly. The biggest drop is usually between the first visit and the second, which is about trust and habit more than design.

    Adoption funnel: what moves weekly users? Try it

    Each change moves one or two stages of the funnel (the effects are illustrative). Notice which changes help people find the page, which help them come back, and which one makes things worse.

    Measure it like a product

    Power BI, Tableau and Looker all log who viewed what and when. Pull those logs into the warehouse and track weekly active viewers and the week-2 return rate, the same way you would for an app.

    -- report_views(user_id, report_id, viewed_at): one row per page view
WITH weekly AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT user_id, DATE_TRUNC('week', viewed_at) AS week
    FROM report_views
    WHERE report_id = 'weekly-sales'
),
first_seen AS (
    SELECT user_id, MIN(week) AS first_week
    FROM weekly
    GROUP BY user_id
)
SELECT
    f.first_week,
    COUNT(*)                                   AS new_viewers,
    COUNT(w.user_id)                           AS back_next_week,
    ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(w.user_id) / COUNT(*), 1) AS return_pct
FROM first_seen f
LEFT JOIN weekly w
       ON w.user_id = f.user_id
      AND w.week = f.first_week + INTERVAL '7 days'
GROUP BY f.first_week
ORDER BY f.first_week;

    The trust loop

    Key ideas
    • Adoption is a funnel: aware, opened, returned, weekly active, used in decisions.
    • The first visit is easy to win; the second depends on trust and habit.
    • Numbers that match finance and one certified definition per metric build trust.
    • Launch inside an existing routine, such as a weekly meeting or a scheduled email.
    • Retire the old report, or people will keep using it.
    Common mistakes
    • Announcing a dashboard once by email and calling it launched.
    • Leaving the old spreadsheet running, so two versions of revenue compete.
    • Answering every request by adding another chart until the page serves nobody.
    • Never looking at usage logs, so dead reports pile up for years.
