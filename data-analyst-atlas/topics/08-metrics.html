Business metrics & KPIs

A business runs on a handful of numbers: how many people convert, how many stay, what each customer costs and what each one is worth. This area teaches you to define those numbers precisely, read them as funnels, cohorts and unit economics, and trace a surprising change back to its cause.

In this area

    Every lesson here follows one running example: a subscription habit-tracking app with a free sign-up and a monthly paid plan. People arrive, sign up, start paying, keep paying or leave, and each paying month earns revenue. You first learn to define a metric so everyone computes it the same way. Then you read the customer journey three ways: as a funnel (who converts), as cohorts (who stays) and as unit economics (whether each customer pays back what it cost to win them). Finally you pick one north star with guardrails, and use a metric tree to explain why a number moved.

    08.1 · Definitions first

    Designing good metrics

    A metric is a definition plus a query. "Active users" sounds obvious until two teams compute it and get 41,000 and 58,000, because one counts app opens and the other counts check-ins, one includes staff accounts and one uses a rolling seven days. Good metrics are written down so precisely that anyone with the data gets the same number.

    Why it matters for data analysts

    Most "why don't these numbers match?" tickets are definition problems, not math problems. When you own the definitions, you stop arguing about numbers and start discussing what they mean.

    The anatomy of a definition

    Every count of people answers four questions: what one unit is (a user, an account, an order), which logged event qualifies, over which time window, and who is excluded. Leave any one out and two people will fill it in differently.

    Precise, measurable, actionable

    Use three tests. Precise: the definition leaves no choices open. Measurable: the event is actually logged, at a grain and freshness you have. Actionable: when it moves, someone knows what to do. A metric that fails the third test is a vanity metric: it looks good on a slide but cannot guide a decision.

    Metric definition checker Try it

    The app's events land in the warehouse once a night, so an hourly metric cannot be computed from it. Feelings are not logged at all. Those are measurability failures, and no amount of SQL fixes them.

    Pick the right kind of number

    TypeExampleWatch out for

    RateActivation: first check-in within 7 days ÷ sign-upsState the denominator and the window explicitly
    AverageRevenue per paying user (ARPPU)A few big spenders drag it; show the median beside it

    Also label each metric leading or lagging. Revenue lags: by the time it falls, customers have already left. Activation and weekly check-ins lead: they move first and you can still act on them.

    -- Weekly active subscribers: the written definition, as code
SELECT
  date_trunc('week', e.event_ts AT TIME ZONE 'UTC') AS week,   -- weeks start Monday
  u.platform,
  COUNT(DISTINCT e.user_id)                         AS weekly_active_subscribers
FROM events e
JOIN users u ON u.user_id = e.user_id
WHERE e.event_name = 'checkin_completed'
  AND u.is_test = FALSE
  AND u.is_staff = FALSE
  AND u.plan = 'paid'
GROUP BY 1, 2
ORDER BY 1, 2;
    Key ideas
    • A metric is unit + event + window + filters, written down with an owner and a source table.
    • Test every metric: precise, measurable, actionable.
    • Cumulative totals and raw counts flatter; rates per period reveal change.
    • Pair a lagging outcome (revenue) with leading inputs (activation, weekly usage).
    Common mistakes
    • Counting events and calling it users: one keen user with 40 check-ins becomes 40 "active users".
    • Leaving the time zone or week start undefined, so two dashboards disagree at every boundary.
    • Quietly changing a definition without versioning it, which creates a fake jump in the trend.
    • Reporting a rate without its denominator, so nobody notices when the denominator shrinks.

    08.2 · Where people drop out

    Funnels & conversion

    A funnel follows the same people through an ordered series of steps and asks what share makes it from each step to the next. It turns "we need more customers" into "we lose 92% of visitors before they sign up", which tells you where to look.

    Why it matters for data analysts

    Funnels are the most requested analysis in product and marketing teams. Getting the steps, the order and the conversion window right is what separates a funnel people trust from one that starts arguments.

    Step conversion and overall conversion

    Step conversion is the share of people at one step who reach the next. Overall conversion is the share of the first step who reach the last, and it equals all the step rates multiplied together. Drop-off is simply one minus the step rate.

    \[ \text{step}_k = \frac{n_{k+1}}{n_k}, \qquad \text{overall} = \frac{n_{\text{last}}}{n_1} = \prod_k \text{step}_k \] Example: \(0.08 \times 0.50 \times 0.40 \times 0.50 = 0.008\), so 0.8% of visitors end up paying.

    Order and conversion windows

    A funnel needs two more decisions. Does order matter (must the trial come before the payment)? And how long does someone have to convert? A 14-day window means a payment on day 21 does not count for this cohort. Fix the cohort by its first step: "September sign-ups who paid within 14 days" is stable, while "September payments divided by September sign-ups" mixes different people.

    Funnel builder Try it

    Because the steps multiply, the same number of extra points is worth far more on a low rate. Moving 8% to 12% is a 50% lift; moving 50% to 54% is an 8% lift.

    -- September sign-up cohort: trial and paid within 14 days, in order
WITH steps AS (
  SELECT
    u.user_id,
    MIN(e.event_ts) FILTER (WHERE e.event_name = 'trial_started') AS trial_ts,
    MIN(e.event_ts) FILTER (WHERE e.event_name = 'first_payment') AS paid_ts
  FROM users u
  LEFT JOIN events e
    ON  e.user_id  = u.user_id
    AND e.event_ts >= u.signup_ts
    AND e.event_ts <  u.signup_ts + INTERVAL '14 days'
  WHERE u.signup_ts >= '2026-09-01' AND u.signup_ts < '2026-10-01'
    AND NOT u.is_test
  GROUP BY u.user_id
)
SELECT
  COUNT(*)                                              AS signups,
  COUNT(trial_ts)                                       AS trials,
  COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE paid_ts > trial_ts)            AS paid,
  ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(trial_ts) / COUNT(*), 1)          AS signup_to_trial_pct,
  ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE paid_ts > trial_ts)
        / NULLIF(COUNT(trial_ts), 0), 1)                AS trial_to_paid_pct
FROM steps;
    Key ideas
    • Step conversions multiply into overall conversion.
    • Define the funnel by a cohort at step one, an order rule and a conversion window.
    • Absolute drop-off shows where most people leave; relative lift shows where a fix pays most.
    • Segment funnels by channel, platform and device before drawing conclusions.
    Common mistakes
    • Dividing this month's payments by this month's sign-ups, which mixes different cohorts.
    • Counting events instead of distinct users at each step, so a step can exceed the one before it.
    • Reading a recent cohort before its window has closed and seeing a fake drop.
    • Optimizing the last step only, when most people leave at the first.

    08.3 · Who stays

    Cohorts & retention

    A cohort is a group of users who started in the same period, such as everyone who first paid in January. Retention asks what share of that group is still active one, two or six months later. Tracking cohorts separately stops new arrivals from hiding the fact that old ones are leaving.

    Why it matters for data analysts

    Total active users can grow while every cohort decays, as long as marketing keeps pouring people in. Cohort tables show whether the product keeps people, which drives lifetime value and almost every growth forecast.

    Reading a cohort table

    Each row is a cohort, each column is the age of the cohort in months, and each cell is the share still active. Read across a row to see one cohort age. Read down a column to compare cohorts at the same age. Read a diagonal to see one calendar month across all cohorts, which is where outages and seasonality show up.

    The shape of the curve

    Plot one row as a line and look at where it ends. A curve that flattens means a core group finds lasting value. A curve that keeps decaying toward zero means every cohort eventually leaves, and growth depends on constant new acquisition. A smile means some users come back later, often after a new feature or a seasonal need.

    Cohort retention heatmap generator Try it

    Pick a retention definition

    DefinitionCounts a user at day or month N if…Good for
    N-day (bounded)Active exactly in period NHabit products used regularly
    Unbounded (rolling)Active in period N or any later periodIrregular use, like travel or tax apps
    Subscription retentionStill paying at the start of period NPaid plans; churn rate = 1 − retention per period
     
    import pandas as pd

# payments: one row per user per paid month (user_id, paid_at)
p = payments.copy()
p["month_idx"]  = p["paid_at"].dt.year * 12 + p["paid_at"].dt.month
p["cohort_idx"] = p.groupby("user_id")["month_idx"].transform("min")
p["age"]        = p["month_idx"] - p["cohort_idx"]          # 0, 1, 2, ...
p["cohort"]     = p.groupby("user_id")["paid_at"].transform("min").dt.to_period("M")

counts = (p.groupby(["cohort", "age"])["user_id"].nunique()
            .unstack(fill_value=0))
retention = counts.div(counts[0], axis=0).round(3)          # divide by cohort size
print(retention.iloc[:, :7])
    Key ideas
    • Group users by start period and track each group separately.
    • Rows show ageing, columns compare cohorts, diagonals show calendar events.
    • The level where the curve flattens drives lifetime value more than the first drop.
    • State whether retention is bounded, unbounded or paid.
    Common mistakes
    • Averaging recent cohorts with old ones for late months, where only the old cohorts have data.
    • Calling growing total actives "good retention" while each cohort decays.
    • Comparing cohorts of very different sizes or channel mixes as if they were alike.
    • Mixing up monthly churn with annual churn: 5% a month is about 46% a year, not 60%.

    08.4 · Does each customer pay off?

    Unit economics (CAC, LTV, margin)

    Unit economics asks one question about a single customer: do they earn back more than they cost to win and serve? If the answer is yes, growth makes money. If it is no, every new customer digs the hole deeper, however good the sign-up chart looks.

    Why it matters for data analysts

    Finance, marketing and leadership all quote CAC, LTV and payback, often computed three different ways. You are the person who can say exactly which spend, which customers and which margin went into each number.

    The four numbers

    Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is marketing and sales spend divided by new paying customers in the same period. Contribution margin per month is revenue per user (ARPU) times gross margin, the money left after the cost of serving them. Lifetime value (LTV) is that margin over the expected lifetime, which for a steady monthly churn rate is margin divided by churn. Payback is how many months of margin it takes to recover CAC.

    \[ \text{CAC} = \frac{\text{spend}}{\text{new customers}}, \quad m = \text{ARPU} \times \text{GM}, \quad \text{LTV} = \frac{m}{\text{churn}}, \quad \text{payback} = \frac{\text{CAC}}{m} \] Example: $30,000 spend ÷ 200 customers = $150 CAC. $15 ARPU × 80% margin = $12 a month. With 6% monthly churn, LTV = $12 ÷ 0.06 = $200, so LTV:CAC ≈ 1.3 and simple payback = 12.5 months.

    Blended CAC hides bad channels

    Blended CAC divides all spend by all new customers, including the ones who arrived for free. Paid CAC leaves out organic customers. Per-channel CAC is where decisions get made, because one expensive channel can hide inside a healthy average.

    CAC, LTV and payback calculator Try it

    A common rule of thumb in subscription businesses is LTV:CAC of about 3 or more and payback within roughly 12 months. Treat both as conventions to discuss, not laws: a business with cheap capital can accept longer payback, and an LTV built on a guessed churn rate is only as good as that guess.

         A                              B
1    Marketing spend (month)        30000
2    New paying customers           200
3    ARPU per month                 15
4    Gross margin                   80%
5    Monthly churn                  6%
7    CAC                            =B1/B2              → 150
8    Margin per customer per month  =B3*B4              → 12
9    LTV                            =B8/B5              → 200
10   LTV : CAC                      =B9/B7              → 1.33
11   Simple payback (months)        =B7/B8              → 12.5
12   Churn-adjusted payback         =IF(B9<=B7,"never",LN(1-B7/B9)/LN(1-B5))   → 22.4
    Key ideas
    • CAC = spend ÷ new customers; say whether it is blended, paid or per channel.
    • Use margin, not revenue, in LTV and payback.
    • LTV = monthly margin ÷ monthly churn when churn is steady; small churn changes swing it a lot.
    • Payback with churn is longer than simple payback, and never comes if LTV is below CAC.
    Common mistakes
    • Computing LTV from revenue instead of margin, which inflates it by the cost of serving.
    • Using blended CAC to defend a channel whose own CAC is far above LTV.
    • Dividing this month's spend by this month's customers when sales cycles last months.
    • Estimating churn from a young cohort, then projecting LTV years into the future.

    08.5 · One goal, with brakes

    North star & guardrail metrics

    A north star metric is the single number that best captures the value customers get from the product, chosen so that growing it also grows the business. Guardrail metrics are the numbers you promise not to break while pushing it. Together they let a team move fast without winning the metric and losing the customer.

    Why it matters for data analysts

    Teams optimize what they are measured on. You often propose the north star, define its inputs and guardrails, and are the first to notice when a "win" is really a cost moved somewhere else.

    Inputs above, guardrails below

    A north star is usually a lagging outcome, so teams work on input metrics that drive it: how many people reach value, how often and how deeply they use it. Guardrails sit underneath and catch the side effects: unsubscribes, margin, support load, page speed.

    Goodhart's law in action

    "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Suppose the team is told to raise sessions per user and turns on daily push notifications. Sessions jump. Unsubscribes jump more, and a few weeks later the weekly active base starts to shrink. Without a guardrail on unsubscribes, the dashboard calls this a win for a month.

    North star vs guardrails Try it

    The model behind the demo is illustrative: each lever lifts the north star with diminishing or steady returns, and each one also pushes on a guardrail. Real responses come from experiments, which is the next area.

    Key ideas
    • A north star measures delivered customer value and links to long-term revenue.
    • Teams move input metrics; the north star summarizes them.
    • Guardrails have explicit limits agreed before the push, not after.
    • Any single target can be gamed; pair it with the cost it might shift.
    Common mistakes
    • Picking revenue as the north star, which lags and says little about customer value.
    • Picking an activity count, like sessions, that can rise while value falls.
    • Listing ten guardrails, so every change breaks one and the team ignores them all.
    • Checking guardrails only at launch, when harm such as churn shows up weeks later.

    08.6 · Why did it move?

    Metric trees & root-cause analysis

    A metric tree writes a top-line number as the product or sum of smaller numbers, and those as smaller numbers again. When revenue drops, you walk down the tree, following the branch that moved most, until you reach something specific enough to fix: "Android checkout conversion fell after Tuesday's release."

    Why it matters for data analysts

    "Revenue is down 12%, why?" is one of the most common and most urgent questions you will get. A tree plus a fixed routine turns a stressful hunt into a structured answer within hours.

    Build the tree from identities

    Each split must be an exact identity, so the children multiply or add back to the parent. Multiplicative splits break a number into drivers: revenue = orders × average order value (AOV). Additive splits break it into segments: sessions = search + organic + email + social. This lesson uses an online store, the classic case.

    Attribute the change

    For a product, the percentage changes do not add up, but their logarithms do. That gives each driver an exact share of the change. For a sum, each segment's share is just its own change divided by the total change.

    \[ \ln\frac{R_1}{R_0} = \ln\frac{O_1}{O_0} + \ln\frac{A_1}{A_0}, \qquad \text{share}_{\text{orders}} = \frac{\ln(O_1/O_0)}{\ln(R_1/R_0)} \] Example: revenue −11.7% (ln −0.124), orders −10.8% (ln −0.114), AOV −1.0% (ln −0.010). Orders explain 92% of the drop.

    A routine for root cause

    Metric tree drill-down: revenue fell 11.7% this week Try it
    Click a node (or focus it and press Enter) to open its children. 

    -- Which platform explains the change in orders between two weeks?
WITH w AS (
  SELECT
    platform,
    SUM(orders) FILTER (WHERE week = DATE '2026-11-09') AS prev,
    SUM(orders) FILTER (WHERE week = DATE '2026-11-16') AS curr
  FROM weekly_orders
  GROUP BY platform
)
SELECT
  platform, prev, curr,
  curr - prev                                                    AS change,
  ROUND(100.0 * (curr - prev) / SUM(curr - prev) OVER (), 1)     AS pct_of_total_change
FROM w
ORDER BY change;
    Key ideas
    • Split metrics only by exact identities: × for drivers, Σ for segments.
    • Follow the branch that explains the most change; log shares for products, plain shares for sums.
    • Rule out data problems and known events before analysing behaviour.
    • A root cause is specific, sized and timed: what changed, where, how much, since when.
    Common mistakes
    • Chasing the segment with the largest percentage change when it is too small to matter.
    • Missing a mix shift: every segment is flat, but traffic moved toward a low-converting one, so the total fell.
    • Stopping at "conversion dropped", which restates the problem instead of explaining it.
    • Skipping the data check and spending a day explaining a broken pipeline.

    Put it together

    Projects for this area

    Starter

    A metric spec sheet

    Pick an app or store you know and write definitions for five metrics: unit, event, window, filters, owner and source. Turn each into a SQL query against an invented schema, and mark each one leading or lagging.

    Intermediate

    Funnel and cohort report

    Take any public e-commerce or app event log. Build a cohort-based funnel with a conversion window and a monthly retention triangle, then write one page on where users drop out and whether newer cohorts retain better.

    Stretch

    Root-cause drill

    Generate a synthetic weekly orders table by channel and platform, plant a hidden drop (for example, one platform's conversion falls), and swap with a peer. Find theirs with a metric tree, size it with contribution shares, and write the incident summary.
