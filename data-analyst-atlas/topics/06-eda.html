Exploratory data analysis

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) is how you get to know a dataset before you trust it with a decision. You sharpen the question, look at each column on its own, then at pairs of columns, segments and time, and you write down what you find. Most "why did this number move?" requests are answered with nothing more than this loop done carefully.

In this area

    EDA starts with a question and ends with findings that someone can act on. In between you walk the same data, an online store's orders table, from several angles: one column at a time, two columns together, split by segment and laid out over time. Each angle can send you back to sharpen the question. The six lessons follow that path, and the last one turns it into a repeatable workflow.

    06.1 · Start sharp

    Asking the right questions

    "How are sales doing?" has no answer you can check. "What was net revenue from first-time customers in the last 4 weeks, compared with the 4 weeks before?" does. EDA goes well or badly depending on how sharp the question is before you open the data.

    Why it matters for analysts

    Stakeholders usually arrive with a worry, not a question. Turning that worry into something measurable is the most valuable thing you do all week. It stops you from making 40 charts that answer nothing and gives you a clear point at which the analysis is done.

    Climb the ladder from vague to measurable

    A sharp question names five things: the metric (exactly which number), the population (whose rows count), the time window, a comparison (against what), and the decision the answer will feed. Each rung below adds one of them.

    Break "why?" into pieces you can measure

    "Why did revenue fall?" is too big to answer directly, so split it into parts that multiply back together. Revenue is orders times average order value (AOV), and orders are sessions times conversion rate. Each leaf of the tree is a number you can pull from the data, and the leaf that moved tells you where to dig next.

    Sharpen the question Try it

    Watch the traps. "Customer happiness" is not in the orders table, "our best customers" has no definition, competitors' revenue is not data you hold, and "to prove it worked" decides the answer before you look.

    Vague versus sharp, side by side

    Vague requestSharp versionWhat made it measurable
    "Is the new checkout working?"Did checkout conversion on mobile change in the 14 days after release vs the 14 days before?Metric, segment, window, baseline
    "Who are our best customers?"Which 10% of customers by 12-month net revenue, and what share of revenue do they bring?Defined population and threshold
    "Did the email campaign help?"Was 30-day repeat-purchase rate higher for emailed customers than the held-out group?Metric, comparison group
    "Why are support tickets up?"Which ticket categories grew most per 1,000 orders this month vs last month?Normalized rate, breakdown

    Once the question is sharp, the first query almost writes itself:

    -- Net revenue from first-time customers: last 4 weeks vs the 4 weeks before
WITH first_orders AS (
  SELECT customer_id, MIN(order_date) AS first_date
  FROM orders
  GROUP BY customer_id
)
SELECT
  CASE WHEN o.order_date >= CURRENT_DATE - 28 THEN 'last_4w' ELSE 'prior_4w' END AS period,
  SUM(o.order_value - o.refund_amount) AS net_revenue,
  COUNT(*)                             AS orders
FROM orders o
JOIN first_orders f
  ON f.customer_id = o.customer_id AND f.first_date = o.order_date
WHERE o.order_date >= CURRENT_DATE - 56
  AND o.order_date <  CURRENT_DATE
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;
    Key ideas
    • A sharp question names a metric, a population, a time window, a comparison and a decision.
    • Split "why did X move?" into factors that multiply back to X, then check which factor moved.
    • Agree on the question with the stakeholder before you query anything.
    • A question that can only have one welcome answer is not a question.
    Common mistakes
    • Starting with "let's see what the data says" and producing charts nobody asked for.
    • Comparing a number with nothing, so you cannot tell whether it is good or bad.
    • Using a metric whose definition differs by team ("revenue" with or without refunds and tax).
    • Framing the question to confirm what the requester already believes.

    06.2 · Shape before summary

    One variable at a time

    Before you relate columns to each other, look at each one alone. What values does it take, where is the middle, how spread out is it, and is anything impossible? A histogram of order_value answers more of these in one glance than any single number can.

    Why it matters for analysts

    Most reporting mistakes start here: quoting a mean for skewed money data, missing a spike caused by a pricing rule, or not noticing negative quantities and test orders. Five minutes of univariate checks per column saves a retraction later.

    Read the shape first

    Order values are right-skewed: lots of small baskets and a long tail of big ones. In skewed data the mean is pulled toward the tail, so the median describes a "typical" order better. Shapes also carry business stories. The bump just above $50 here is customers topping up their basket to reach free shipping.

    A box plot in one line

    A box plot squeezes the same column into five numbers: the quartiles Q1, median and Q3, plus whiskers. The box holds the middle 50% of orders, its width is the interquartile range (IQR = Q3 − Q1). Points beyond Q3 + 1.5 × IQR are drawn one by one as possible outliers. These are rows to look at, not rows to delete.

    Histogram bin width Try it

    Wide bins hide the free-shipping bump inside a big bar; very narrow bins turn the chart into noise. Try a few widths and keep the one that shows real features. Then add the bulk orders and watch the mean jump while the median hardly moves.

    What to check for each column type

    Column typeLook atRed flags
    Money, counts (skewed)Histogram, median, P90/P99, maxNegatives, round-number spikes, $0 test orders
    Roughly symmetric numbersHistogram, mean, standard deviationTwo humps (two populations mixed)
    Categories (channel, device)Bar chart of counts, share of "other"/nullTypos, a category at 90%+, new labels
    DatesMin, max, counts per dayGaps, future dates, everything on the 1st
    IDsCount vs distinct countDuplicates where the grain should be unique
     
    import pandas as pd
import seaborn as sns

orders = pd.read_csv("orders.csv", parse_dates=["order_date"])

v = orders["order_value"]
print(v.describe(percentiles=[.1, .25, .5, .75, .9, .99]))
print("skew:", round(v.skew(), 2))                # > 1 means strongly right-skewed
print("negative or zero:", (v <= 0).sum())

q1, q3 = v.quantile([.25, .75])
fence = q3 + 1.5 * (q3 - q1)
print(orders.loc[v > fence].sort_values("order_value").tail())   # inspect, don't drop

print(orders["channel"].value_counts(dropna=False, normalize=True))
sns.histplot(v[v < 200], binwidth=5)            # try several bin widths
    Key ideas
    • Look at the shape (histogram) before you trust any summary number.
    • For skewed data, report the median and percentiles; the mean is pulled by the tail.
    • Bin width changes what a histogram shows, so try several.
    • Box-plot outliers are rows to investigate, not rows to delete.
    • Every column type has its own checklist: ranges, nulls, distinct counts, odd spikes.
    Common mistakes
    • Reporting "average order value" from the mean without checking skew or a few huge orders.
    • Using the default bin count and missing a spike or a second hump.
    • Running value_counts() without dropna=False, so missing values disappear.
    • Dropping every point beyond the 1.5 × IQR fence as "bad data".

    06.3 · Two columns together

    Relationships between variables

    Once each column makes sense, you ask how pairs of columns move together. Do customers with more sessions spend more? Do mobile orders have smaller baskets? The kind of chart and the summary number you use depend on what types the two columns are.

    Why it matters for analysts

    Relationships are where hypotheses come from: "discounts drive bigger baskets", "slow delivery hurts repeat purchases". A single correlation coefficient can hide a curve, a cluster or one huge customer, so you plot first and quote numbers second.

    Match the chart to the pair of types

    Two numbers call for a scatter plot. A category and a number call for side-by-side box plots or bars of the median. Two categories call for a crosstab, often shaded as a heat map. A date and a number call for a line, which is the next-but-one lesson.

    Correlation measures straight-line togetherness

    The Pearson correlation \(r\) runs from −1 to +1 and measures how well a straight line fits the cloud. Spearman's \(\rho\) is Pearson computed on ranks, so it captures any steadily rising or falling pattern and shrugs off extreme values.

    \[ r = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_i (y_i - \bar{y})^2}} \] Each point contributes the product of its distances from the two means. One point far from both means can contribute more than all the others together.
    Scatter, trend line and correlation Try it

    Lower the true link to almost nothing, then add the whale. The trend line swings toward that one point and Pearson's \(r\) jumps, while Spearman's \(\rho\), which only looks at ranks, barely moves.

    cust = (orders.groupby("customer_id")
             .agg(spend=("order_value", "sum"), sessions=("sessions_30d", "max")))

print(cust[["sessions", "spend"]].corr(method="pearson"))
print(cust[["sessions", "spend"]].corr(method="spearman"))   # rank-based, robust

import seaborn as sns
sns.regplot(data=cust, x="sessions", y="spend", scatter_kws={"alpha": .4})

# category x number: compare medians, not just means
print(orders.groupby("device")["order_value"].median())

# category x category: row percentages
print(pd.crosstab(orders["channel"], orders["device"], normalize="index").round(2))
    Key ideas
    • Column types choose the chart: scatter, grouped box plots, crosstab or line.
    • Pearson \(r\) measures straight-line fit; Spearman \(\rho\) measures any steady rank order.
    • A curve can have \(r \approx 0\), and one extreme point can create a large \(r\).
    • Correlation is a lead for a hypothesis, not proof that one column causes the other.
    Common mistakes
    • Quoting a correlation without plotting the scatter.
    • Reading \(r = 0\) as "no relationship" when the pattern is curved.
    • Concluding causation: active customers spend more, but maybe big spenders browse more.
    • Comparing raw counts in a crosstab instead of row or column percentages.

    06.4 · Split, then compare

    Segmentation & cohorts

    An average over everyone hides the groups inside it. Split the orders by channel, device, region or customer type and the "flat" overall number often turns out to be one segment growing and another shrinking. A cohort is a special segment: customers grouped by when they started, followed over time.

    Why it matters for analysts

    Nearly every useful finding is a segment finding: "the drop is all mobile", "paid social brings low-value customers", "the March cohort retains better". Segments also protect you from the classic trap where the overall comparison points the opposite way from every segment.

    Split, apply, combine

    Segmenting is three moves. Split the rows by a column, apply the same metric to each group, then combine the results into one table you can compare. Always keep each segment's size next to its rate, because a dramatic rate on 40 orders is mostly noise.

    Simpson's paradox: when the total lies

    Suppose a new checkout (B) converts better than the old one (A) on mobile and on desktop. B can still look worse overall if more of B's traffic happens to be mobile, which converts lower for everyone. This is Simpson's paradox: a difference in segment mix flips the aggregate comparison. The cure is to compare within segments, or to weight both groups to the same mix.

    Segment split and Simpson's paradox Try it

    A always gets 10,000 visitors, half on mobile. B also gets 10,000, and the slider sets how many of them are on mobile. B wins on each device at every setting (2.4% vs 2.0% on mobile, 5.5% vs 5.0% on desktop). Push B's mobile share up and watch the overall bars.

    Cohorts: segments defined by a start date

    A cohort table puts the signup month on each row and "months since signup" across the columns. Reading along a row shows how one cohort decays; reading down a column compares cohorts at the same age, which is fair. Mixing ages, for example comparing January's total repeat rate with April's, is not.

    -- Cohort table: share of each signup month's customers who order N months later
WITH firsts AS (
  SELECT customer_id, DATE_TRUNC('month', MIN(order_date)) AS cohort
  FROM orders GROUP BY customer_id
), activity AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT f.cohort, o.customer_id,
         (EXTRACT(YEAR FROM AGE(DATE_TRUNC('month', o.order_date), f.cohort)) * 12
          + EXTRACT(MONTH FROM AGE(DATE_TRUNC('month', o.order_date), f.cohort)))::int AS month_n
  FROM orders o JOIN firsts f USING (customer_id)
)
SELECT cohort, month_n,
       COUNT(*) * 100.0 / FIRST_VALUE(COUNT(*)) OVER (PARTITION BY cohort ORDER BY month_n) AS pct_active
FROM activity
GROUP BY cohort, month_n
ORDER BY cohort, month_n;
    # Split-apply-combine in pandas, with segment sizes kept beside the rates
seg = (orders.groupby("channel")
             .agg(orders=("order_id", "nunique"), revenue=("order_value", "sum"))
             .assign(aov=lambda d: d.revenue / d.orders,
                     share=lambda d: d.orders / d.orders.sum())
             .sort_values("revenue", ascending=False))
print(seg.round(2))
    Key ideas
    • Split, apply, combine: the same metric per segment, side by side, with sizes.
    • A change in segment mix can move the total even when no segment changed.
    • Simpson's paradox: the aggregate can point the opposite way from every segment.
    • Cohorts group customers by start date so you compare them at the same age.
    Common mistakes
    • Reporting a segment's dramatic rate without its tiny sample size.
    • Slicing by 30 dimensions until something looks significant by chance.
    • Comparing an old cohort's lifetime total with a young cohort's total.
    • Explaining a total change without checking whether the mix of segments shifted.

    06.5 · Trend, cycle, noise

    Patterns over time

    Plot almost any business metric by day and you see a mix of things layered together: slow growth, a weekly rhythm, a holiday season, one-off spikes and random wobble. Before you call a change "real", you need to know which layer moved.

    Why it matters for analysts

    "Revenue is down 4% this month" is the most common alarm you will field, and a large share of these alarms are the calendar: fewer weekend days, a holiday moving week, or a promo last year. Separating the layers keeps you from chasing ghosts.

    Pull the series apart

    A daily series can be read as trend × seasonality × residual. Trend is the slow drift. Seasonality repeats on a fixed period: weekly for most stores, yearly for holidays. The residual is what is left, including spikes such as a promo day. For sales, the parts usually multiply: a Saturday is "17% above a normal day", whatever the level.

    Smooth away the week, compare like with like

    A 7-day rolling average contains exactly one of each weekday, so the weekly rhythm cancels out and the trend shows through. For month or year comparisons, align by weekday: compare with 364 days ago (52 weeks) rather than 365, and compare per day or per weekday-adjusted day rather than monthly totals.

    Daily revenue: cycles and smoothing Try it

    The readout's "weekday swing" measures how much of the weekly zig-zag survives in the smoothed line. Try windows of 5, 6, 7 and 10 days. Only some window lengths cancel the cycle exactly. The cost of smoothing is lag: a 28-day average reacts to a real change weeks late.

    daily = (orders.set_index("order_date")
               .resample("D")["order_value"].sum())        # one row per day, gaps filled with 0

roll7 = daily.rolling(7).mean()                           # weekly cycle cancels out

dow_index = daily.groupby(daily.index.dayofweek).mean() / daily.mean()
adjusted = daily / daily.index.dayofweek.map(dow_index)   # weekday-adjusted series

yoy = daily / daily.shift(364) - 1                        # same weekday, 52 weeks ago

from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import STL
parts = STL(daily, period=7, robust=True).fit()           # trend, seasonal, resid
parts.plot()
    Key ideas
    • A daily series layers trend, weekly and yearly seasonality, and residual noise or events.
    • A rolling window that is a multiple of 7 days removes the weekly cycle; other lengths leave some behind.
    • Compare like with like: per day, weekday-aligned (364 days back) or weekday-adjusted.
    • Smoothing adds lag, so use it to see trend, not to spot sudden breaks.
    Common mistakes
    • Comparing monthly totals of months with different lengths or weekend counts.
    • Comparing this week with last week when last week held a promo or a holiday.
    • Missing days in the data, so rolling(7) silently spans more than a week.
    • Calling a single residual spike a new trend.

    06.6 · Make it repeatable

    An EDA workflow

    Good EDA looks creative but runs on a checklist. You agree the question, check that the table is what you think it is, profile each column, look at pairs, segments and time, and write every finding down with its evidence. The order matters: a segment comparison on a table full of duplicates is worse than no comparison.

    Why it matters for analysts

    A repeatable workflow makes you fast on unfamiliar data and makes your work reviewable. When a stakeholder asks "did you check for duplicates?" you can point at the line in your findings log instead of re-running the notebook in a panic.

    Seven steps, one loop

    The first two steps are gates. Until you know the grain (what one row means: one order, one order line, one customer per day) and have checked duplicates, nulls and ranges, every later number is suspect. After that, explore freely, but log as you go.

    Guided EDA checklist on the orders table Try it

    The buttons are shuffled on purpose. Run a check before the ones it depends on and its finding is logged with a warning, because the number it produced cannot be trusted yet.

    Keep a findings log

    Write each finding as one sentence plus its evidence, your confidence and what to do next. A log turns a wandering notebook into something a reviewer can check and a stakeholder can act on, and it becomes the outline of your write-up.

    import pandas as pd

def profile(df: pd.DataFrame, key: str) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """One row per column: type, nulls, distinct values, min and max."""
    print(f"rows={len(df):,}  distinct {key}={df[key].nunique():,}")   # grain check
    out = pd.DataFrame({
        "dtype":    df.dtypes.astype(str),
        "null_pct": df.isna().mean().mul(100).round(1),
        "distinct": df.nunique(),
        "min":      df.min(numeric_only=False),
        "max":      df.max(numeric_only=False),
    })
    return out.sort_values("null_pct", ascending=False)

orders = pd.read_csv("orders.csv", parse_dates=["order_date"])
print(profile(orders, key="order_id"))
dupes = orders[orders.duplicated("order_id", keep=False)]
print(len(dupes), "rows share an order_id")
    Key ideas
    • Agree the question, then pass the quality gate (grain, duplicates, nulls, ranges) before exploring.
    • Explore in widening circles: one column, pairs, segments, time.
    • Log each finding with evidence, confidence and a next step as you go.
    • Reuse a profiling function so every new table gets the same first look.
    Common mistakes
    • Jumping to segment charts before confirming one row means one order.
    • Keeping findings in your head, then forgetting how you got a number.
    • Exploring forever: stop when the agreed question is answered.
    • Presenting every chart you made instead of the three that matter.
    In practice

    Automated profilers such as ydata-profiling produce a full report in one line. They are a good start for step 3, but they do not know your grain or your question, so steps 1, 2 and 7 stay with you.
