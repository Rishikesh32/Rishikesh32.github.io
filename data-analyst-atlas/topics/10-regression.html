Regression & statistical modeling

Regression turns "what drives this number?" into a model you can read: each coefficient says how much the outcome moves when one input changes and the others stay put. You will fit lines, read coefficients in dollars and percent, check whether the model can be trusted, predict yes/no outcomes and pick between competing models without fooling yourself.

In this area

    Every regression project in this area uses one running example: an online store's orders table, with order value, number of items, discount, customer tenure and device. You start by fitting a straight line (10.1), learn to read what its coefficients mean (10.2), and check the residuals to see whether the model can be trusted (10.3). When the outcome is yes or no, you switch to logistic regression (10.4). Then you engineer features and watch for overfitting (10.5), and finally compare candidate models on data they have not seen (10.6).

    10.1 · The best straight line

    Linear regression

    Linear regression draws the straight line through a cloud of points that makes the vertical misses as small as possible. For the store's orders it answers questions like "how much bigger is an order, on average, for each extra item in the basket?" with one number you can read in dollars.

    Why it matters for analysts

    Most "what drives X?" questions (order value, support handle time, campaign revenue) start with a regression. It gives a size for each driver, a measure of uncertainty and a baseline forecast, and every fancier model you meet later is judged against it.

    The line and its misses

    The model is \(\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\): an intercept \(\beta_0\) (the prediction when \(x = 0\)) plus a slope \(\beta_1\) (the change in \(\hat{y}\) per one-unit change in \(x\)). For each order, the residual \(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\) is how far the real value sits above or below the line.

    Ordinary least squares (OLS) picks the one line that minimizes the sum of squared errors (SSE). Squaring makes misses above and below count the same and punishes big misses much more than small ones.

    \[ \hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x} \] The slope is the covariance of \(x\) and \(y\) divided by the variance of \(x\). The line always passes through the point of means \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\), here (4.5 items, $67.13).
    Beat least squares (you can't) Try it

    Move your line with the sliders and watch your SSE. Then drag a point: drag the 8-item order down to $20 and the least-squares line tilts hard, because one big squared miss outweighs many small ones. That sensitivity is why outliers deserve a look before you trust a slope.

    How good is the fit? R²

    Compare the line to the laziest model, which predicts the mean order value for everyone. Its total squared miss is the total sum of squares (SST). The line removes part of it (SSR, the explained part) and leaves the rest as SSE. \(R^2\) is the share removed.

    \[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \frac{\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2} \] With one predictor, \(R^2\) equals the squared correlation \(r^2\). It says how much variation the line accounts for, not whether the line is the right shape or whether \(x\) causes \(y\).

    More than one predictor

    Multiple regression adds predictors: \(\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_p x_p\). The fitting rule stays the same (minimize SSE), and in matrix form the solution is \(\hat{\beta} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y\). Software does this for you. Your job is choosing the columns and reading the output.

    import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

orders = pd.read_csv("orders.csv")   # order_id, order_value, items, discount_pct, tenure_months, device

model = smf.ols("order_value ~ items", data=orders).fit()
print(model.params)        # Intercept and slope (12.43 and 12.15 on the 8-order example)
print(model.rsquared)      # share of variance explained

orders["fitted"] = model.fittedvalues
orders["resid"] = model.resid          # y minus y-hat, used for diagnostics in 10.3

# multiple regression: same call, more columns
multi = smf.ols("order_value ~ items + discount_pct + tenure_months", data=orders).fit()
print(multi.summary())
    -- PostgreSQL has least-squares aggregates built in (one predictor only)

    Explaining

    You care about the coefficients: "an extra item adds about $12". You need sensible controls, honest standard errors and residual checks (10.2, 10.3).

    Predicting

    You care about error on new orders: "forecast next month's basket size". You judge the model on held-out data, not on R² (10.5, 10.6).

    Key ideas
    • OLS picks the coefficients that minimize the sum of squared residuals.
    • The slope is the average change in \(\hat{y}\) per one-unit change in \(x\); the line passes through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\).
    • R² is the share of variation around the mean that the model explains.
    • Squared errors make OLS sensitive to outliers and extreme points.
    Common mistakes
    • Reading a high R² as proof that the model is right or that \(x\) causes \(y\).
    • Interpreting the intercept when \(x = 0\) never happens (no order has zero items).
    • Predicting far outside the range of \(x\) you fitted on.
    • Never plotting the data, so one extreme order silently drives the slope.

    10.2 · What the numbers mean

    Interpreting coefficients

    A coefficient is the change in the predicted outcome for a one-unit change in one predictor, holding the other predictors in the model fixed. Three things decide what that sentence really says: the units of each variable, which controls are in the model, and whether you logged anything.

    Why it matters for analysts

    Stakeholders will quote your coefficient in a meeting. "Discounts lower order value by $0.49 a point" and "discounts raise it by $0.37" can both come from the same table, depending on the controls. Reading coefficients correctly is the difference between a useful answer and a confident wrong one.

    Reading a regression table

    Here is a fit on 500 example orders with four predictors. Each row is one coefficient with its standard error (how much it would wobble across samples), a p-value and a 95% confidence interval. The interval is the most useful column: it gives a plausible range for the effect in real units.

    Controls change the answer

    Regress order value on discount alone and you get −$0.49 per discount point. It looks like discounts shrink baskets. The trouble is that discount codes mostly go to new customers, and new customers spend less anyway. Tenure is a confounder: it drives both who gets a discount and how much they spend.

    Add tenure and basket size as controls and the coefficient flips to +$0.37. Now it compares orders from customers with the same tenure, items and device. This is called omitted variable bias: leave out a variable that affects the outcome and is correlated with your predictor, and the predictor absorbs its effect.

    Controls are not free. Never control for something your predictor causes (a mediator, such as "items added after seeing the discount banner"), because that removes part of the effect you want to measure. And even a well-controlled coefficient is an association. To know what a discount causes, you run an experiment (Area 09).

    Units and log transforms

    Order values are right-skewed and effects are often proportional: an extra item adds more to a $200 basket than to a $20 one. Taking logs turns those proportional effects into straight lines and lets you read coefficients as percentages.

    ModelRead \(\beta\) asExample (500 orders, with controls)
    level-level: \(y \sim x\)+1 unit of \(x\) → \(+\beta\) units of \(y\)+1 item → +$9.68
    log-level: \(\ln y \sim x\)+1 unit of \(x\) → about \(100\beta\%\) change in \(y\) (exactly \(e^{\beta}-1\))\(\beta = 0.145\): +1 item → +15.6%
    level-log: \(y \sim \ln x\)+1% in \(x\) → \(+\beta/100\) units of \(y\)\(\beta = 24.8\): +1% items → +$0.25
    log-log: \(\ln y \sim \ln x\)+1% in \(x\) → \(+\beta\%\) in \(y\) (an elasticity)\(\beta = 0.39\): +10% items → about +3.9%
    dummy: \(y \sim\) C(device)difference from the reference levelmobile vs desktop: −$6.98
    \[ \ln(\text{value}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{items} + \dots \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\text{value}(\text{items}+1)}{\text{value}(\text{items})} = e^{\beta_1} \] With \(\beta_1 = 0.145\), \(e^{0.145} = 1.156\): one more item multiplies the expected order value by 1.156, a 15.6% increase. The "\(100\beta\%\)" shortcut works only for small \(\beta\) (below about 0.1).
    Coefficient interpreter Try it
    Control for:

    Start with discount and no controls, then add controls one at a time. Which single control moves the discount coefficient most? Then switch the outcome to log and read the same effect as a percentage.

    import numpy as np
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

level = smf.ols("order_value ~ items + discount_pct + tenure_months + C(device)",
                data=orders).fit()
print(level.params["discount_pct"], level.conf_int().loc["discount_pct"].values)

# log outcome: coefficients become (approximate) percentage effects
logm = smf.ols("np.log(order_value) ~ items + discount_pct + tenure_months + C(device)",
               data=orders).fit()
pct = (np.exp(logm.params) - 1) * 100        # exact % change per one-unit increase
print(pct.round(1))                           # items ≈ 15.6, discount_pct ≈ 0.6 in the example

# log-log: the items coefficient is an elasticity
elastic = smf.ols("np.log(order_value) ~ np.log(items) + tenure_months", data=orders).fit()
    Key ideas
    • A coefficient is a "one more unit, others held fixed" comparison, in the units of \(y\) per unit of \(x\).
    • Adding or dropping a correlated control can change a coefficient's size and even its sign.
    • Log outcome: \(e^{\beta} - 1\) is the percentage change. Log-log: \(\beta\) is an elasticity.
    • Dummy coefficients are differences from the reference category.
    • Report the confidence interval, not just the point estimate or the p-value.
    Common mistakes
    • Saying "discounts cause bigger orders" from an observational regression.
    • Comparing raw coefficient sizes across predictors measured in different units.
    • Controlling for a mediator and then concluding the treatment "has no effect".
    • Using \(100\beta\%\) for a large log coefficient (0.7 is +101%, not +70%).

    10.3 · Can you trust it?

    Assumptions & diagnostics

    OLS will fit a line to anything. Whether the coefficients, intervals and p-values mean what they claim depends on a few assumptions about the residuals. The quickest way to check them is to plot the residuals and look for any pattern at all.

    Why it matters for analysts

    A curved relationship fitted with a straight line gives wrong effects at both ends. Uneven spread makes your confidence intervals too narrow, so you report "significant" drivers that are not. Five minutes of residual plots before you present saves the awkward follow-up meeting.

    What the model assumes

    A useful memory aid is LINE. Each assumption fails in a recognisable way, and most failures show up in one plot: residuals against fitted values. A healthy plot is a shapeless horizontal band around zero.

    AssumptionWhat goes wrong if it failsHow to checkUsual fix
    LinearityBiased coefficients and predictionsCurve in residuals vs fittedTransform, add \(x^2\) or splines
    IndependenceStandard errors too smallResiduals over time; repeated customersClustered SEs, time-series models
    Normal errorsSmall-sample intervals off (large n is forgiving)Q-Q plot of residualsLog the outcome, bootstrap
    Equal varianceIntervals too narrow or too wideFunnel in residuals vs fittedLog the outcome, robust (HC3) SEs
    Residual plot clinic Try it

    The readout measures two things: the spread ratio (residual size in the top third of fitted values divided by the bottom third; near 1 is healthy) and a curvature score (how much of the residual variation a U-shape explains; near 0 is healthy). Try each fix on each dataset. Robust standard errors change the uncertainty, not the picture.

    Influential points

    Not every odd point matters equally. A point with an unusual \(x\) value has high leverage: it sits at the end of the seesaw and can tilt the whole line. Cook's distance combines leverage and residual size into one number per row. A common rule of thumb flags points above \(4/n\).

    Multicollinearity

    When two predictors move together (items and basket weight, say), the model cannot tell their effects apart. Predictions stay fine, but the individual coefficients get huge standard errors and can flip sign between samples. The variance inflation factor measures it: regress predictor \(j\) on the others and compute \(\text{VIF}_j = 1/(1-R_j^2)\). Values above about 5 to 10 deserve attention.

    import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
from statsmodels.stats.diagnostic import het_breuschpagan
from statsmodels.stats.outliers_influence import variance_inflation_factor
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

m = smf.ols("order_value ~ items + discount_pct + tenure_months", data=orders).fit()

plt.scatter(m.fittedvalues, m.resid, s=8); plt.axhline(0, color="grey")   # look first
lm_stat, lm_p, f_stat, f_p = het_breuschpagan(m.resid, m.model.exog)    # small p: unequal variance

cooks = m.get_influence().cooks_distance[0]
print(orders.loc[cooks > 4 / len(orders), ["order_id", "items", "order_value"]])

X = m.model.exog                                     # includes the intercept column
print([variance_inflation_factor(X, i) for i in range(1, X.shape[1])])

robust = m.get_robustcov_results(cov_type="HC3")      # same coefficients, honest SEs
print(robust.summary())
    Key ideas
    • Plot residuals against fitted values for every model; a shapeless band is the goal.
    • A curve means the model's shape is wrong: transform or add terms.
    • A funnel means unequal variance: log the outcome or use robust standard errors.
    • High-leverage points can tilt the line; Cook's distance finds them.
    • Correlated predictors inflate standard errors; check VIF.
    Common mistakes
    • Checking whether the raw outcome is normal. The assumption is about the residuals.
    • Deleting outliers to improve R² without finding out what they are.
    • Treating many orders from the same customer as independent rows.
    • Dropping a predictor because its p-value rose when a correlated one was added.

    10.4 · Yes or no outcomes

    Logistic regression

    When the outcome is yes or no (did the session end in a purchase, did the customer churn, was the order returned), you want a probability between 0 and 1. Logistic regression fits a straight line on the log-odds scale and bends it through an S-shaped curve so every prediction stays a valid probability.

    Why it matters for analysts

    Conversion, churn, repeat purchase and fraud flags are all binary. Logistic regression gives you a probability per customer, an odds ratio per driver that stakeholders can understand, and a model you can explain line by line, which is why it remains the default before any machine learning.

    Why not a straight line?

    Here are 80 example store sessions: pages viewed against whether the visitor bought (1) or not (0). A straight line through 0/1 data (a linear probability model) eventually predicts probabilities below 0 or above 1. The logistic curve flattens out at both ends instead.

    \[ \ln\frac{p}{1-p} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x)}} \] \(p/(1-p)\) is the odds. The model is linear in the log-odds, so each extra unit of \(x\) adds \(\beta_1\) to the log-odds and multiplies the odds by \(e^{\beta_1}\), the odds ratio.

    Three scales for the same prediction

    Coefficients live on the log-odds scale, which nobody thinks in. Convert them. With \(\beta_1 = 0.288\), each extra page multiplies the odds of buying by \(e^{0.288} = 1.33\), a 33% increase in the odds. That is not a 33% increase in probability: how much the probability moves depends on where you start.

    Logistic curve, threshold and odds ratio Try it

    Move \(\beta_1\) and watch the odds ratio. Press Fit to let the computer find the coefficients with the lowest log loss, the average of \(-\ln p\) for buyers and \(-\ln(1-p)\) for non-buyers. Then slide the threshold: it does not change the model, only which sessions you act on.

    From probabilities to decisions

    To act (send a coupon, flag for review) you pick a threshold. Above it you predict "yes". Lowering it catches more real buyers (higher recall) at the cost of more false alarms (lower precision). The right threshold depends on what each mistake costs, not on 0.5 by default. Area 12 covers ROC curves and calibration in depth.

    import numpy as np
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# sessions: one row per visit, purchased is 0/1
logit = smf.logit("purchased ~ pages_viewed + C(device)", data=sessions).fit()
print(logit.summary())

odds_ratios = np.exp(logit.params)              # pages_viewed ≈ 1.33 in the example
ci = np.exp(logit.conf_int())                   # interval for each odds ratio

sessions["p_buy"] = logit.predict(sessions)     # probabilities, always in (0, 1)
sessions["flag"] = sessions["p_buy"] >= 0.3    # threshold chosen from costs

# average marginal effect: change in probability per extra page, in points
print(logit.get_margeff().summary())
    Key ideas
    • Logistic regression is a linear model for the log-odds, squashed into a probability by the sigmoid.
    • \(e^{\beta}\) is the odds ratio: the multiplier on the odds for one more unit of \(x\).
    • The same odds ratio moves probabilities by different amounts depending on the starting point.
    • Coefficients are fitted by maximum likelihood (minimum log loss), not least squares.
    • The threshold is a business decision applied after the model, trading precision for recall.
    Common mistakes
    • Reading an odds ratio of 1.33 as "33% more likely". It is 33% higher odds.
    • Fitting ordinary linear regression to a 0/1 outcome and reporting probabilities above 1.
    • Judging the model by accuracy when only 3% of sessions convert.
    • Using 0.5 as the threshold without asking what false positives and false negatives cost.

    10.5 · Shaping the inputs

    Features, interactions & overfitting

    A regression only knows the columns you give it. Turning categories into dummies, letting one effect depend on another and adding curves all make the model more flexible. Past a point, extra flexibility starts fitting noise, and the model gets worse on orders it has not seen.

    Why it matters for analysts

    "Do discounts work better on mobile?" is an interaction question. "Does spend level off for loyal customers?" is a curve question. Knowing how to encode both, and how to tell a real pattern from an overfit one, is most of the craft in applied regression.

    Categories become dummy columns

    A text column such as device cannot enter a formula directly. One-hot (dummy) encoding makes one 0/1 column per category and leaves one category out as the reference. Each dummy coefficient is then the difference from that reference. Keep all \(k\) dummies plus an intercept and the columns become perfectly collinear (the "dummy variable trap").

    Interactions: when one effect depends on another

    Without an interaction, the model forces the discount effect to be the same on every device. Adding the product term discount_pct × is_mobile lets each device have its own slope. The interaction coefficient is the difference between the two slopes.

    \[ \widehat{\text{value}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{disc} + \beta_2\,\text{mobile} + \beta_3\,(\text{disc} \times \text{mobile}) \] Desktop slope: \(\beta_1\). Mobile slope: \(\beta_1 + \beta_3\). With an interaction in the model, \(\beta_1\) alone no longer means "the" discount effect.

    Curves and the overfitting trap

    Spend often rises quickly for new customers and levels off. A straight line in tenure misses that bend; adding \(\text{tenure}^2\) (a polynomial term), a log or a spline captures it. Each added term gives the model more freedom. Training error always falls as you add terms, but error on new data falls, bottoms out, then climbs. That climb is overfitting: the model memorises noise.

    Polynomial degree vs train and validation error Try it

    Training customers (filled) fit the curve; validation customers (hollow) are held back to judge it. Raise the degree and watch the two error curves on the right split apart. The degree you want is where validation error is lowest, not training error.

    Taming flexibility

    Three habits keep models honest. Hold out data (or cross-validate) and judge every change on it. Add terms because the residuals or the business logic ask for them, not because they are available. And when you have many predictors, use regularization: ridge regression shrinks all coefficients toward zero, and lasso can set weak ones exactly to zero.

    import numpy as np
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures, StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, RidgeCV
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

# dummies, an interaction and a curve, all in one formula
m = smf.ols("order_value ~ discount_pct * C(device, Treatment('desktop'))"
            " + tenure_months + I(tenure_months ** 2)", data=orders).fit()

# pick the polynomial degree by cross-validated error, not training error
X, y = orders[["tenure_months"]], orders["order_value"]
for degree in range(1, 7):
    pipe = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(degree), StandardScaler(), LinearRegression())
    rmse = -cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5, scoring="neg_root_mean_squared_error").mean()
    print(degree, round(rmse, 2))

# many features: let ridge shrink them, with the penalty chosen by CV
ridge = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), RidgeCV(alphas=np.logspace(-3, 3, 13)))
    Key ideas
    • Categorical predictors enter as \(k-1\) dummy columns; coefficients compare to the reference level.
    • An interaction term lets one predictor's effect depend on another; its coefficient is the difference in slopes.
    • Polynomials, logs and splines let a linear model fit curves.
    • Training error always falls with flexibility; validation error shows when you start overfitting.
    • Regularization (ridge, lasso) trades a little bias for much less variance.
    Common mistakes
    • Reading a main-effect coefficient as "the" effect when an interaction is in the model.
    • Adding a high-degree polynomial because it raises R².
    • Choosing features or the degree on the same data you report performance on.
    • Encoding categories as integers (desktop = 1, mobile = 2, tablet = 3), which invents an order.

    10.6 · Picking a winner

    Comparing models

    You rarely have one model; you have five candidates with different features. Raw R² cannot choose between them, because adding any column, even a column of random numbers, never lowers it. You need measures that charge a price for complexity or test the model on rows it did not see.

    Why it matters for analysts

    The model you present becomes "the" answer about what drives order value. Picking it by R² rewards whoever added the most columns. Adjusted R², AIC and cross-validation give you a defensible reason to prefer the simpler model, and a number to show when someone asks "why not add X?"

    Penalise complexity: adjusted R², AIC, BIC

    Adjusted R² only rises when a new predictor improves the fit by more than chance alone would. AIC and BIC trade fit (via SSE) against the number of coefficients \(k\); lower is better, and only differences between models fitted to the same rows mean anything. BIC charges more per coefficient, so it prefers smaller models.

    \[ R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - (1 - R^2)\,\frac{n-1}{n-p-1}, \qquad \text{AIC} = n \ln\frac{\text{SSE}}{n} + 2k, \qquad \text{BIC} = n \ln\frac{\text{SSE}}{n} + k \ln n \] \(n\) rows, \(p\) predictors, \(k = p + 1\) coefficients including the intercept. The AIC and BIC shown are for linear regression with normal errors, up to a constant that cancels when you compare models. A rule of thumb: an AIC gap under 2 is a near tie.

    Test on unseen rows: cross-validation

    k-fold cross-validation splits the rows into \(k\) groups. Each group takes one turn as the validation set while the model is fitted on the other \(k-1\), and you average the \(k\) errors. Every row is used for validation exactly once, so the estimate is steadier than one train/test split, and it measures what you care about when predicting: error on new orders, in dollars.

    Model comparison bench Try it

    Each track spans the worst to best value over all 256 possible feature sets, with the better end on the right. Add the three noise columns to a good model: the R² dot nudges right while the other three slide left. Can you find the single best model by cross-validated error?

    Which measure when?

    MeasureRewardsUse it whenWatch out
    R²Fit onlyDescribing one modelNever use it to choose between models of different size
    Adjusted R²Fit, light penaltyQuick check of nested linear modelsStill in-sample; weak penalty
    AICFit vs \(2k\)Choosing among models on the same rows, aiming to predictOnly differences matter; same \(y\) and rows
    BICFit vs \(k \ln n\)You want the smallest adequate explanatory modelCan underfit when effects are many and small
    Cross-validated errorPrediction on unseen rowsPrediction is the goal; any model typeTime-ordered data needs time-based folds

    For explanation, also ask whether the coefficients you care about are stable across reasonable models. If the discount effect is +$0.37 in every sensible specification, you can say so with confidence; if it swings with each choice, report that too. Keep a final test set untouched until the very end so the number you present was not used to choose the model.

    import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, cross_val_score

specs = {
    "A": "order_value ~ items + tenure_months + discount_pct + C(device)",
    "B": "order_value ~ items + I(items ** 2) + tenure_months + discount_pct + C(device)",
    "E": "order_value ~ items + I(items ** 2) + tenure_months + discount_pct + C(device)"
         " + day_of_month + zip_digit + email_opens",
}
for name, f in specs.items():
    m = smf.ols(f, data=orders).fit()
    print(name, round(m.rsquared, 4), round(m.rsquared_adj, 4), round(m.aic, 1), round(m.bic, 1))

# 5-fold cross-validated RMSE for the same feature sets
cv = KFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
orders["items_sq"] = orders["items"] ** 2
orders["is_mobile"] = (orders["device"] == "mobile").astype(int)
cols_b = ["items", "items_sq", "tenure_months", "discount_pct", "is_mobile"]
rmse_b = -cross_val_score(LinearRegression(), orders[cols_b], orders["order_value"],
                          cv=cv, scoring="neg_root_mean_squared_error").mean()
    Key ideas
    • R² never decreases when you add predictors, so it cannot choose between models.
    • Adjusted R², AIC and BIC penalise extra coefficients; compare them only across models fitted to the same rows.
    • Cross-validation estimates error on unseen data directly and works for any model.
    • Prefer the simplest model that is within noise of the best score.
    • For explanation, check that key coefficients are stable across sensible specifications.
    Common mistakes
    • Comparing AIC between a model of order value and a model of log order value.
    • Comparing models fitted on different rows because one dropped missing values.
    • Using random folds on time-ordered data, which leaks the future into training.
    • Reporting the cross-validated score of the model you picked as if it were an untouched test score.
