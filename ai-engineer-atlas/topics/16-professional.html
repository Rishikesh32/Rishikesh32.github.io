Professional skills

The field changes every month, so the engineers who thrive are the ones who can read a paper quickly, filter news without drowning, tie models to real user problems, explain trade-offs to people who do not care about F1 scores, and show their work. These skills compound for your whole career.

In this area

    Treat these five skills as one loop. You learn from papers and releases, decide what is worth building for real users, explain the trade-offs so others can agree, and show the result in work people can inspect. Each turn of the loop makes the next one faster.

    16.1 · Learn from the source

    Reading papers

    Papers are where new techniques show up first, months before tutorials. You do not need to read every word of every paper. You need a fast way to decide which ones deserve your time, and a method for pulling out what matters from the few that do.

    Why it matters for AI engineers

    Model cards, technical reports and arXiv preprints are the primary sources behind every model, retrieval trick and eval method you use. Engineers who can read them judge claims themselves instead of trusting summaries, and can reproduce a result before betting a product on it.

    The three-pass method

    S. Keshav's three-pass method reads a paper in rounds, each deeper than the last, with a decision after each. Most papers stop after pass one, and that is the point.

    Pass one ends with the five Cs: category (what kind of paper), context (what it builds on), correctness (do the assumptions look valid), contributions (what is new) and clarity (is it well written). If you cannot fill them in, the paper is outside your area or badly written.

    Where to look in an ML paper

    ML papers share a shape, so you can jump to the parts that carry the evidence. The abstract makes the claim, the main results table tests it, and the ablations show which component actually earns the gain.

    Read the main table skeptically. Are the baselines recent and tuned? Do all rows use similar compute and data? Is there more than one seed, and is the gain larger than the noise? A 0.3-point gain on a benchmark that varies by 0.5 between runs is not a result.

    Reproducing a result

    Reproduction is the deepest form of reading. Start from the authors' code if it exists, match their config exactly, and run several seeds before you compare. Write down every gap between paper and code; those gaps are often where the real trick hides.

    import json
import random

import numpy as np
import torch

def set_seed(seed):
    random.seed(seed); np.random.seed(seed)
    torch.manual_seed(seed); torch.cuda.manual_seed_all(seed)

config = {"lr": 3e-4, "batch_size": 64, "epochs": 10, "paper_value": 71.3}

scores = []
for seed in (0, 1, 2):
    set_seed(seed)
    scores.append(train_and_eval({**config, "seed": seed}))   # your training + eval function

mean, std = float(np.mean(scores)), float(np.std(scores))
print(f"reproduced {mean:.1f} ± {std:.1f} vs paper {config['paper_value']}")
json.dump({"config": config, "scores": scores}, open("repro.json", "w"), indent=2)

    If you land within the reported variance, you understand the paper. If not, check preprocessing, the evaluation script and the appendix hyperparameters before blaming the authors.

    Key ideas
    • Read in three passes with an exit after each; most papers deserve only the first.
    • Finish pass one by writing the five Cs and a one-line summary.
    • The evidence lives in the main table and the ablations, not the abstract.
    • Gains smaller than seed-to-seed variance are noise.
    • Reproducing a result, with several seeds and a logged config, is the deepest understanding.
    Common mistakes
    • Reading linearly from page one and running out of time before the experiments.
    • Trusting a headline number without checking baselines, compute and seeds.
    • Reading papers with no notes, so nothing is findable a month later.
    • Reproducing with one seed and declaring the paper wrong.

    16.2 · Filter the firehose

    Keeping up

    Something new ships every week, and nobody can follow all of it. Keeping up is a filtering problem: choose a few high-signal sources, give them a fixed time budget, and turn what you read into notes and small experiments so it sticks.

    Why it matters for AI engineers

    Providers deprecate models, frameworks change interfaces, and a new technique can halve your cost, so missing news hurts. Chasing every announcement hurts too: it burns hours and makes your stack churn. A deliberate information diet keeps you current without the anxiety.

    A layered information diet

    Put primary sources first: release notes, changelogs, docs and model cards for the tools you actually use, plus papers you triage. Add two or three curated newsletters so someone else filters the long tail. Cap the social feed; it is good for early warnings and poor for depth.

    Keep a one-line note per item, and turn the most promising one into a small experiment each week. Explaining it to your team is the final test of understanding.

    Evaluate claims before you adopt

    Launch posts are marketing. Before you switch models or libraries, ask whether the change solves a problem you have, whether there is evidence beyond the announcement, and how it does on your own eval set.

    An hour with your own eval set beats a week of reading opinions. Public benchmarks measure someone else's task; your 50 to 200 real examples measure yours, including cost and latency.

    Key ideas
    • Keeping up is filtering: few sources, fixed time budget, one triage list.
    • Primary sources (release notes, model cards, papers) beat commentary.
    • Convert reading into notes and one small experiment a week.
    • Judge new releases on your own eval set, then adopt behind a flag.
    Common mistakes
    • Switching models on launch day because of a benchmark chart.
    • Following dozens of newsletters and reading none of them properly.
    • Missing a deprecation notice in your own provider's changelog.
    • Reading about techniques for months without trying one.

    16.3 · Problems before models

    Product sense

    Product sense is the habit of starting from a user's problem instead of a model's capability. Ask what slows the user down and what a wrong answer would cost. Only then ask which technique helps, and whether the simplest one would do.

    Why it matters for AI engineers

    Many failed AI features work fine technically and solve nothing, or automate a task where one mistake costs more than the feature saves. Engineers who frame features around user problems and failure costs choose better scopes, win buy-in faster and ship things people keep using.

    Frame the problem and the cost of failure

    Write the problem as a job: who, doing what, blocked by what, measured how. "Support agents spend 6 minutes finding the right policy per ticket" is a job. "Add a chatbot" is a solution looking for one.

    Then place the task on two axes: how often it happens and how costly a wrong answer is. The quadrant suggests how much autonomy the AI should get.

    \[ \text{error cost per month} = \text{volume} \times \text{error rate} \times \text{cost per error} \] 20,000 tickets × 3% wrong × $15 to fix each = $9,000 a month. Reviewing every ticket at $0.80 costs $16,000, so blanket review loses money; reviewing only flagged cases may win. Section 16.4 has a calculator.

    Track three kinds of metric. If the quality number climbs while the product number stays flat, you optimised the wrong thing.

    Metric typeQuestion it answersExamples
    ProductDid the user's job get easier?task success, time saved, retention, adoption
    QualityIs the output right?accuracy on your eval set, groundedness, edit rate
    GuardrailWhat must not get worse?cost per task, p95 latency, escalations, complaints

    Prioritise, then pick the autonomy level

    With several candidate features, plot impact against effort. Quick wins ship first and build trust. Big bets need a staged plan. Money pits get cut early, however exciting the demo.

    Impact vs effort matrix Try it

    Drag each idea to where you estimate its impact and effort. The examples are features for a customer-support product.

    Start each feature at the lowest autonomy level that delivers value. Move up a rung only when your data shows errors are rare, visible and reversible.

    Key ideas
    • Write the problem as a user's job with a measurable pain before choosing any technique.
    • Frequency × cost of a wrong answer sets how much autonomy the AI gets.
    • Error cost = volume × error rate × cost per error; compare it with the cost of review.
    • Track product, quality and guardrail metrics together.
    • Ship quick wins first, and climb the autonomy ladder only with evidence.
    Common mistakes
    • Starting from "we should use AI" and searching for a problem afterwards.
    • Launching fully autonomous on day one for a task with expensive errors.
    • Celebrating model accuracy while task success and adoption stay flat.
    • Ignoring the non-AI baseline (a rule, a search box, a better form) that might solve it.

    16.4 · Decide in writing

    Communicating trade-offs

    Every AI decision trades quality, latency, cost, risk and effort, and other people have to live with your choice. Communicating trade-offs means writing down the options, comparing them on the criteria your reader cares about, and stating the decision and its consequences in plain numbers.

    Why it matters for AI engineers

    Product, finance, legal and support judge your system by outcomes they understand. A clear design doc gets faster approval, a decision record stops the same debate every quarter, and a well-framed trade-off earns trust when something goes wrong.

    Design docs and decision records

    A design doc is written before building: the problem, goals and non-goals, at least two real options plus "do nothing", a comparison on explicit criteria, the decision and the risks. Non-goals matter as much as goals because they stop scope creep.

    An architecture decision record (ADR) is the one-page version that lives in the repo: context, decision, consequences. When someone asks in six months why you chose this model, the answer is a link.

    # ADR-012: Small model with retrieval for support answers

Status: Accepted, 2026-03-04

## Context
Agents need policy answers in under 2 s at about 40k requests/day.
Options compared in design doc DD-31 (A: small + RAG, B: large, C: fine-tuned).

## Decision
Use A. Route answers with retrieval score below 0.5 to human review.

## Consequences
+ p95 latency 1.1 s; about $16/day at current volume
- 6 points below B on our 300-case eval set; revisit if CSAT drops
- The retrieval index is now a dependency we must monitor

    Show trade-offs with numbers

    Plot the options on the two axes your reader cares about, usually quality against cost or latency. An option that is worse on both axes than another is dominated and can be dropped. The rest form a frontier, and the choice becomes a business question: what is the next point of quality worth?

    The same logic decides human review. Compare what errors cost with what review costs, and review only where it pays.

    Cost of a wrong answer Try it

    \[ C_{\text{none}} = V e c, \qquad C_{\text{all}} = V r + V e c\,(1-k), \qquad C_{\text{flag}} = V f r + V e c\,(1 - \rho k) \] \(V\) volume, \(e\) error rate, \(c\) cost per error, \(r\) review cost per item, \(k\) share of errors a reviewer catches, \(f\) share of items flagged, \(\rho\) share of errors that land in the flagged set. Reviewing everything pays only when \(e\,c\,k > r\).

    Speak the stakeholder's language

    Translate model metrics into user outcomes, money and time. Show two or three real failures next to the numbers; one concrete bad answer persuades more than a percentage.

    Instead ofSay
    "Tagging F1 is 0.87.""About 13 in 100 tickets get a wrong tag; an agent fixes each in about 30 seconds."
    "p95 latency is 2.8 s.""One user in 20 waits about 3 seconds for an answer."
    "Hallucination rate is 2%.""About 1 answer in 50 states something our docs do not say. Here are three examples."
    "We should fine-tune.""Three weeks of work makes answers 4 points better on our test set and cuts cost per request by a third."
    Key ideas
    • Design docs argue from problem to decision; include non-goals and a "do nothing" option.
    • ADRs record context, decision and consequences in the repo so decisions stay findable.
    • Drop dominated options; along the frontier, price each extra point of quality.
    • Human review pays when error rate × cost per error × catch rate exceeds review cost per item.
    • Translate metrics into outcomes, money and time, with real examples.
    Common mistakes
    • Presenting one option and calling it a design doc.
    • Choosing criteria after scoring, so the comparison justifies a decision already made.
    • Reporting F1 or BLEU to executives without saying what it means for users.
    • Hiding known weaknesses, which destroys trust the first time they show up.

    16.5 · Show your work

    Building a portfolio

    A portfolio is evidence that you can take an AI idea from a vague problem to something measured, shipped and explained. Two or three deep projects beat ten notebook demos, because depth is the thing a certificate cannot show.

    Why it matters for AI engineers

    Plenty of people can call an LLM API. What sets an AI engineer apart is evals, production thinking and judgement, and those only show up in projects with real data, measured results and written trade-offs.

    What a strong project shows

    Pick a problem with real users or real data, even if the user is you. Build the simplest baseline first, then an eval set, then iterate against it. Each iteration changes one thing and gets scored.

    Ship it somewhere people can try, and measure latency and cost per request. Finish with a write-up: what you tried, what worked, what failed and why.

    Make the evals visible

    The most convincing artifact is a chart or table of results across iterations on a fixed eval set. It proves you measure instead of guessing.

    import json
import statistics

from my_app import answer          # your system under test

cases = [json.loads(line) for line in open("evals/cases.jsonl")]
results = []
for case in cases:
    out = answer(case["question"])
    results.append({"id": case["id"], "pass": case["expected"].lower() in out.lower()})

rate = statistics.mean(r["pass"] for r in results)
print(f"pass rate {rate:.1%} on {len(cases)} cases")
json.dump(results, open("evals/last_run.json", "w"), indent=2)
assert rate >= 0.75, "below the release bar"   # run in CI on every push

    Keep the eval cases in the repo and run them in CI, so a reviewer can reproduce your numbers with one command. Use the scorecard to find your project's weakest dimension.

    Choosing projects

    Choose projects that each prove a different skill, and go deep in one domain you care about. A merged pull request to a well-known library counts too; it shows you can read and change someone else's code.

    Key ideas
    • Two or three deep projects beat many shallow demos.
    • Baseline → eval set → scored iterations is the core of a credible project.
    • Show results across iterations with cost and latency, and keep evals runnable in CI.
    • Ship something people can try, and write up failures as well as wins.
    Common mistakes
    • Tutorial clones with a new dataset name and no evaluation.
    • A README that lists technologies but never states the problem or the result.
    • Demo links that die a month later because of an expired API key.
    • Showing only cherry-picked successes; reviewers trust honest failure analysis more.

    Put it together

    Projects for this area

    Starter

    Four-week reading habit

    Each week, give five new papers a first pass and write their five Cs, take the best one to a second pass, and publish a short summary. After four weeks, review which sources gave you the papers worth reading.

    Intermediate

    Design doc and ADR for a real feature

    Pick an AI feature at work or in an open-source project. Write a design doc with three options, a criteria table, a cost-of-error analysis for human review, and an ADR for the decision, then get one reviewer's comments.

    Stretch

    Flagship portfolio project

    Ship an end-to-end project with an eval set running in CI, a results chart across iterations, a live demo with measured cost and latency, and an honest write-up of failures. Ask two engineers to review it against the scorecard.
