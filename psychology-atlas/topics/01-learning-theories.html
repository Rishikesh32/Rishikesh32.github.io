Learning theories

How do people and animals pick up new behavior? Three classic answers: by association (Pavlov), by consequences (Skinner) and by watching others (Bandura). Learn each one, then use them to build habits, coach teams and change behavior.

In this area

    Learning is a lasting change in behavior caused by experience. Psychologists found three main routes: your brain links two things that happen together (classical), you repeat what gets rewarded (operant), and you copy what you see others do (social). The animated scene below plays all three.

    01.1 · Learning by association

    Classical conditioning (Pavlov)

    What it is: Learning through association. A neutral stimulus becomes associated with a natural response after repeated pairing.

    Pavlov's Famous Experiment

    Ivan Pavlov discovered this accidentally while studying dogs' digestion. He noticed that dogs began to salivate when they saw the person who usually fed them, even before tasting food. He then systematically paired a bell (neutral stimulus) with food (unconditioned stimulus). After multiple pairings:

    • Before: Bell ring = no salivation
    • After: Bell ring alone = salivation (conditioned response)

    Key Terms

    • Unconditioned stimulus (UCS): Something that naturally triggers a response (food → salivation)
    • Conditioned stimulus (CS): A neutral stimulus paired with the UCS (bell)
    • Unconditioned response (UCR): The natural response to the UCS (salivation)
    • Conditioned response (CR): The learned response to the CS (salivation at the bell)

    Real-World Examples

    • Seeing a dentist's office triggers anxiety (office paired with pain)
    • A song playing during a breakup makes you sad when you hear it later
    • A particular smell reminds you of your grandmother's house (scent + memory)

    Extinction & Recovery

    If you ring the bell without food many times, the conditioned response gradually weakens (extinction). However, it can spontaneously return later (spontaneous recovery)—the learning isn't erased, just suppressed.

    01.2 · Learning from consequences

    Operant conditioning (Skinner)

    What it is: Learning through consequences. Behavior is shaped by what follows it—rewards increase behavior, punishments decrease it.

    B.F. Skinner's Work

    B.F. Skinner built on earlier work by Edward Thorndike (Law of Effect: "behaviors followed by positive consequences are repeated"). He studied rats and pigeons in controlled environments (Skinner boxes) where they learned to press levers or peck buttons for food rewards.

    The Core Principle

    Behavior = Function of Consequences

    • Positive reinforcement: Add something desirable → behavior increases (praise for good work, money for a job)
    • Negative reinforcement: Remove something undesirable → behavior increases (turning off a siren when seatbelt is buckled)
    • Positive punishment: Add something undesirable → behavior decreases (scolding a child)
    • Negative punishment: Remove something desirable → behavior decreases (taking away phone as punishment)

    Schedules of Reinforcement

    How often and when you reward behavior affects how quickly it's learned and how resistant it is to extinction:

    • Fixed ratio: Reward after every Nth behavior (every 5 presses = reward). Creates high response rates.
    • Fixed interval: Reward after a time period (once per minute). Creates a dip after reward, then buildup.
    • Variable ratio: Reward after varying number of behaviors. Creates steady, persistent responding (slot machines!).
    • Variable interval: Reward after varying time periods. Creates steady, persistent responding (checking email).

    Real-World Applications

    • Parenting: Star charts reward good behavior, timeouts remove privileges
    • Work: Bonuses after milestones, verbal praise for effort
    • Training animals: Treats reward desired tricks
    • Habit formation: Consistently rewarding a behavior builds a habit

    Shaping

    You can build complex behaviors by rewarding successive approximations. Want to train a dog to sit? First reward standing still, then reward leaning back, then reward all the way down. This is called shaping—breaking the final goal into achievable steps.

    01.3 · Learning by watching

    Social learning theory (Bandura)

    What it is: Learning through observation and imitation. We learn by watching others and the consequences they face.

    Albert Bandura's Insight

    Bandura showed that we don't just learn from direct experience—we learn by observing others. In his famous Bobo doll experiment, children watched an adult act aggressively toward an inflatable doll. Later, the children imitated the aggression when given the chance—even without being rewarded for it.

    Three Key Components

    • Observation: We notice and attend to others' behavior
    • Mental representation: We create a mental model of what we saw
    • Imitation: We reproduce the behavior if motivated to do so

    When Do We Imitate?

    • We're more likely to copy behavior if the person is similar to us
    • We're more likely to copy if the person has status or is liked
    • We're more likely to copy if we see them being rewarded for the behavior
    • We're less likely to copy if we see them being punished

    Real-World Examples

    • Children learning language and manners by watching parents
    • Adolescents adopting hairstyles and speech patterns from peers
    • Employees learning company culture by watching successful colleagues
    • Media influence: seeing violence on screen can increase aggressive behavior
    • Celebrity endorsements work because we imitate people we admire

    Self-Efficacy

    Bandura also introduced self-efficacy—your belief in your ability to succeed at a task. When you watch someone similar to you succeed, it boosts your self-efficacy ("If they can do it, so can I"). This belief then influences your willingness to try and your persistence.

    Vicarious Learning

    You learn not only from direct consequences but from observing others' consequences. If a coworker gets praised for speaking up in meetings, you're more likely to speak up too—you've learned through observation that this behavior leads to positive outcomes.

    01.4 · Putting it to work

    How to apply learning theories

    These three theories aren't just academic—they're practical tools for changing behavior in yourself and others.

    Use Classical Conditioning To: Create Positive Associations

    • Build brand loyalty: Pair your product with positive emotions (upbeat music in ads, free samples)
    • Reduce anxiety: Gradually pair a feared stimulus with something calming (systematic desensitization for phobias)
    • Develop good habits: Pair a desired behavior with something you already enjoy (morning coffee + meditation)
    • Marketing: Associate your brand with celebrities, colors, or feelings people already like

    Use Operant Conditioning To: Shape and Reinforce Behavior

    • Build habits: Consistently reward the behavior you want until it becomes automatic
    • Manage teams: Use specific, timely praise for desired behaviors; apply consequences for unwanted ones
    • Motivate yourself: Break goals into small steps, reward yourself at each step (shaping)
    • Change others: Be clear about what behavior you want, make consequences immediate and meaningful
    • Know your reinforcement schedule: Variable schedules create persistence (use them for important habits); fixed schedules are faster for quick learning

    Use Social Learning To: Leverage Observation and Modeling

    • Onboard new employees: Pair them with successful colleagues who model desired behavior
    • Build confidence: Show examples of people similar to your target audience succeeding at a task
    • Change culture: Publicly recognize people who embody desired values; make them visible role models
    • Teach children: Model the behavior you want them to adopt (kindness, honesty, learning)
    • Build teams: Let new members observe and learn from experienced ones before assigning independent work

    Combining All Three: A Real Example

    Scenario: You want to build an exercise habit.

    • Classical conditioning: Exercise at the same time, in the same place. Pair it with something you enjoy (music, podcast).
    • Operant conditioning: Reward yourself immediately after (healthy snack, check a habit tracker). Start small—10 minutes—to ensure success and reinforcement.
    • Social learning: Find a friend who exercises; watch their discipline. Join a class where you see others succeeding. Tell people about your goal (social accountability).

    Result: Over weeks, the behavior becomes automatic due to classical conditioning, the reward system reinforces consistency, and seeing others do it removes doubt. That's sustainable behavior change.

    When to Use Each Theory

    Goal Theory Method
    Build emotional associations Classical Conditioning Pair stimulus with feeling (music, color, place)
    Change or shape behavior Operant Conditioning Reward what you want; consequence what you don't
    Build confidence or model behavior Social Learning Show examples; be a role model; create visibility
