Cognitive theories

Cognitive psychology treats the mind like an information processor: things come in, get filtered, stored, combined and turned into decisions. This area covers how children build their picture of the world (Piaget), how information moves through memory, how we solve problems, and where our decisions go predictably wrong.

In this area

    Learning theories explain what changes in behavior. Cognitive theories explain what happens inside the head between a stimulus and a response: attention picks what matters, working memory holds it for a few seconds, long-term memory stores it, and your mental models decide how you understand it.     02.1 · How minds develop

    How we think (Piaget)

    Jean Piaget argued that children are not small adults who know less. They think in a different way that changes through four stages, as they build and rebuild mental models of how the world works.

    Why it matters in practice

    Teachers, parents, product designers and trainers all explain things to people whose mental models differ from their own. Piaget's core idea works for adults too: people understand new information through the models they already have, and you have to meet them there.

    Schemas, assimilation and accommodation

    A schema is a mental model: a packaged idea of how something works, like "dogs bark and have four legs". Learning happens in two ways:

    • Assimilation: new information fits an existing schema, so you add it in. A child who knows "dog" calls a new breed a dog.
    • Accommodation: the new information does not fit, so the schema itself must change. The same child sees a cat and hears "no, that's a cat". Now there are two schemas: dog and cat.

    When assimilation fails you feel confusion (Piaget called it disequilibrium). Resolving it is what moves understanding forward.

    The four stages

    • Sensorimotor: babies learn by touching, looking and mouthing. Object permanence arrives: a hidden toy still exists.
    • Preoperational: language and pretend play bloom, but thinking is egocentric (hard to take another's view) and children fail conservation tasks: they think a tall thin glass holds more water than a short wide one.
    • Concrete operational: children reason logically about real, visible things and understand conservation, but struggle with pure abstraction.
    • Formal operational: teenagers and adults can reason about abstract ideas, test hypotheses and think about what could be.
    What later research changed

    Children often show skills earlier than Piaget predicted, and not everyone reaches formal reasoning in every area. Treat the stages as a useful map of how thinking grows, not a strict timetable.

    Using it

    • Start from what they know. Connect new ideas to an existing schema before adding detail.
    • Use concrete examples before abstractions. Show a worked example, then state the rule.
    • Create productive confusion. A well-chosen puzzle that the old model cannot solve triggers accommodation.

    02.2 · The mind as a processor

    Information processing

    The information-processing view compares the mind to a system with stages: input is noticed, held briefly, stored and later retrieved. Each stage has limits, and most everyday mistakes happen at those limits.

    Why it matters in practice

    Your working memory is tiny, your attention is easily pulled away, and memories are rebuilt every time you recall them. Knowing these limits lets you design slides, study plans and instructions that work with the brain rather than against it.

    The memory model

    • Sensory memory takes in far more than you notice. Attention is the gate: only what you attend to moves on.
    • Working memory is the mental desk where you think. Miller's famous "7 ± 2" is now usually estimated at about 4 chunks.
    • Long-term memory stores facts, skills and experiences. How you encode something (meaning beats repetition) decides whether you can retrieve it.

    Working with the limits

    • Chunking: group items into meaningful units. 1-8-0-0-5-5-5-0-1-2-3 is 11 digits; (1-800) (555) (0123) is three chunks.
    • Cognitive load: too much at once overwhelms working memory. Split instructions into steps and remove irrelevant clutter.
    • Deep encoding: connect new facts to meaning and to what you already know. Explaining an idea in your own words beats rereading it.
    • Retrieval practice: testing yourself strengthens memory more than reviewing does.
    Memory is reconstructive

    You do not play memories back like a video. You rebuild them each time, so details can be filled in, changed or even invented. Confident memories are not always accurate ones.

    02.3 · Getting from stuck to solved

    Problem-solving

    A problem is any gap between where you are and where you want to be with no obvious route across. Cognitive psychologists study the strategies people use to cross it and the mental traps that keep them stuck.

    Why it matters in practice

    Debugging code, planning a project, fixing a process: most work is problem-solving. People who know common strategies and common traps get unstuck faster than people who just try harder.

    A simple loop

    Algorithms and heuristics

    • Algorithm: a step-by-step procedure that guarantees the right answer if followed (a recipe, long division, checking every combination).
    • Heuristic: a rule of thumb that is fast and usually good enough but not guaranteed. Examples: working backwards from the goal, breaking a problem into sub-goals, means-ends analysis (reduce the biggest gap first) and analogies to a problem you have solved before.

    Traps that keep you stuck

    • Mental set: using the method that worked last time even when a simpler one exists.
    • Functional fixedness: seeing an object only for its usual purpose. Using a coin as a screwdriver requires dropping it.
    • Confirmation bias: looking only for evidence that supports your first idea.

    Getting unstuck

    • Restate the problem in different words, or draw it.
    • Step away. Breaks allow incubation, and many people get a sudden insight on return.
    • Ask a different person. Someone without your mental set may see the obvious route.
    • List ten uses of each object or resource you have to beat functional fixedness.

    02.4 · Why smart people choose badly

    Decision-making & biases

    We make thousands of decisions a day, so the brain relies on shortcuts. Those shortcuts are usually helpful and sometimes wrong in predictable ways. These systematic errors are called cognitive biases.

    Why it matters in practice

    Hiring, pricing, investing, medical choices and ordinary arguments are all shaped by bias. You cannot remove your biases, but you can set up situations where they do less damage.

    Two ways of thinking

    Biases worth knowing

    BiasWhat happensExample
    AnchoringThe first number you see pulls later estimates toward it.A "was £200, now £120" tag makes £120 feel cheap.
    Availability heuristicEasily remembered events seem more common.After a plane-crash news story, flying feels riskier than driving.
    Confirmation biasYou seek and trust evidence that agrees with you.Reading only the reviews that support the product you already want.
    Loss aversionLosses hurt about twice as much as equal gains please.Keeping a losing stock to avoid "taking the loss".
    Sunk costYou continue because of what you already spent.Finishing a bad film because you paid for the ticket.
    Framing effectThe same facts feel different when worded differently."90% survive" sounds better than "10% die".

    Making better decisions

    • Slow down on big choices. Deliberately engage System 2: write the options down.
    • Consider the opposite. Ask "what would make me wrong?" to counter confirmation bias.
    • Set the anchor yourself. Research a fair number before the other side names theirs.
    • Judge future cost only. Ask "if I were starting fresh today, would I choose this?" to cut sunk cost.
    • Use checklists and base rates. Look up how often something really happens instead of relying on vivid memories.
